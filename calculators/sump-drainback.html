    Visualization

    How the sump drain-back calculator works

    In most aquaponic and recirculating systems one pump in the lowest tank (the sump) lifts water up to the fish tanks and grow beds, and everything else flows back down by gravity. While the pump runs, some water is always “in flight”: a thin layer flowing over each overflow, water standing in flooded beds and channels, and water filling the pipes. When the pump stops (a power cut, a tripped breaker, a blocked intake or maintenance) all of that water drains down into the sump. If the sump does not have room for it, the sump overflows.

    The calculator adds up that drain-back volume, source by source, and compares it with the free space in the sump between the running water level and the rim. It then tells you how high the sump will fill, how much spare room is left, and the band of running water levels that keeps the pump intake covered while the pump runs and still leaves room for drain-back when it stops.

    Why it matters

    The FAO aquaponics manual (Somerville et al., 2014) puts the sump at the lowest point of the system, where the water from the grow beds collects by gravity before the pump returns it to the fish tank, and treats sump sizing as part of the design. Power failures are among the most common emergencies in recirculating systems, which is why the SRAC fact sheet on managing recirculating systems calls backup power essential (Masser et al., 1999). Even with backup power, a pump can fail or be switched off for cleaning. A sump that floods every time the pump stops wets the floor and electrics, loses water and, in a small system, can leave the fish tank low. A sump run too low, on the other hand, lets the pump suck air when it restarts and the water is pumped back up. This calculator helps you find a level that avoids both.

    Formulas

    All lengths are converted to metres and all volumes to litres before the sums (1 m³ = 1,000 L). “× n” is the count of identical items.

    Tank with an overflow, weir or standpipe. While the pump runs, the water surface stands a little above the overflow lip, by the head h needed to push the flow over it. When the flow stops, the tank drains down to the lip, so only that top layer leaves:

    Rectangular tank A = L × W Round tank A = π × D² ÷ 4 Drain-back V = A × h × n

    Tank or bed that drains completely (outlet at the bottom with nothing to stop it, such as a bed drained through an open bottom fitting): use the full water depth as h in the same formula.

    Media bed (flood & drain). Only the space between the media particles holds water. With porosity φ (the pore fraction of the bed):

    V = L × W × flooded depth × φ × n

    DWC canal. V = L × W × d × n, where d is the depth of water above the canal’s outlet. With an overflow standpipe that is just the head over the standpipe (a centimetre or two); with an outlet at the bottom it is the whole water depth.

    NFT channels. The thin nutrient film in each channel drains out. With the film width w and film depth t:

    V = channels × channel length × w × t

    Pipes. A pipe of inside diameter di and length ℓ holds:

    V = π × di² ÷ 4 × ℓ × n

    Count the pump’s return line (it drains back through the pump unless a check valve holds it) and drain lines that sit above the sump water level. The calculator treats them as full, which is the cautious case. Schedule 40 PVC inside diameters are outside diameter − 2 × minimum wall from ASTM D1785, the same values as the Water Velocity and Head Loss calculators.

    Back-siphon through the return line. If the pump’s return pipe ends below the water surface in a tank, the pipe stays full when the pump stops and works as a siphon: water flows backwards through the pump into the sump until the falling water surface uncovers the pipe outlet and air gets in (White, 2011). That can be far more water than everything else combined. With the drop s measured from the overflow lip down to the outlet (or down to a siphon-break hole):

    V = Atank × s × n

    Sump. With the sump floor area As, rim depth H, running depth drun and safety margin m:

    Free capacity F = As × (H − drun) Total drain-back V = Σ Vi Spare (or short) F − V Recommended free V × (1 + m) Level after dafter = drun + V ÷ As Highest run level dmax = H − V × (1 + m) ÷ As Operating band dmin ≤ drun ≤ dmax

    dmin is the lowest safe depth you enter for the pump. The sump level is at its lowest while the pump runs, because that is when the drain-back water is up in the system. If dmax is below dmin there is no safe running level: you need a bigger sump, a smaller drain-back (siphon breaks, shorter or smaller pipes), or both.

    How high is the water over an overflow?

    Measure it if you can: mark the water line on the tank with the pump on, switch the pump off, and measure how far the level falls. For a rough estimate on a free-falling overflow lip or standpipe top, the Francis formula for a sharp-crested weir without end contractions (U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, 2001) can be turned around:

    Q = 1.84 × Lw × h^1.5 (SI: Q in m³/s, Lw and h in m) h = (Q ÷ (1.84 × Lw))^(2/3)

    Lw is the length of the overflow edge; for a round standpipe it is the circumference π × D. For example, 1,500 L/h (0.000417 m³/s) over the top of a 2″ Sch 40 standpipe (ID 52.5 mm, rim length 0.165 m) gives h = (0.000417 ÷ (1.84 × 0.165))2/3 ≈ 0.012 m, about 1.2 cm. Treat this as an estimate only: the formula was developed for larger, straight weirs, and comb overflows, strainers and fittings that run partly full all hold the level higher.

    Siphon breaks and check valves

    A siphon break is a small hole drilled in the return pipe just below the normal water line in the tank. While the pump runs, the hole just squirts a little water. When the pump stops and the level falls to the hole, air rushes in, the water column in the pipe separates, and the siphon stops. Only the water between the overflow lip and the hole drains back. The hole can clog with algae, snails or biofilm, so check it regularly and test it by switching off the pump. A check valve on the pump outlet also stops back-flow, but valves can stick or leak, so it is common practice not to rely on one alone when sizing a sump.

    Example

    The default inputs describe a small media-bed aquaponic system:

    • Fish tank, round, 120 cm across, running 2 cm above its standpipe: A = π × 1.2² ÷ 4 = 1.131 m², V = 1.131 × 0.02 = 22.62 L.
    • Two media beds, 120 × 100 cm, flooded 25 cm deep, 40% pore space: V = 1.2 × 1.0 × 0.25 × 0.40 × 2 = 240 L.
    • Return line, 1″ Sch 40 (ID 1.049″ = 26.64 mm), 4 m: V = π × 0.02664² ÷ 4 × 4 = 2.23 L.
    • Drain line, 2″ Sch 40 (ID 2.067″ = 52.50 mm), 3 m: V = π × 0.0525² ÷ 4 × 3 = 6.49 L.
    • Back-siphon: the return pipe ends 5 cm below the overflow lip in the fish tank, with no siphon break: V = 1.131 × 0.05 = 56.55 L.

    Total drain-back = 22.62 + 240 + 2.23 + 6.49 + 56.55 = 327.9 L. The sump is 120 × 80 cm (As = 0.96 m²), 60 cm deep to the rim and runs at 25 cm, so its free capacity is 0.96 × 0.35 = 336 L. The water fits with only 8.1 L to spare: the level rises 327.9 ÷ 0.96 = 34.2 cm to 59.2 cm, less than a centimetre below the rim. With a 25% margin the sump should have 327.9 × 1.25 = 409.9 L free, so the highest safe running level is 60 − 409.9 ÷ 9.6 = 17.3 cm (9.6 L per cm of depth). The pump needs at least 15 cm, which leaves a narrow operating band of 15–17.3 cm. Drilling a siphon break just below the fish tank’s water line would remove most of the 56.55 L back-siphon and widen that band by almost 6 cm.

    Typical values (rules of thumb)

    ItemTypical value
    Water height over an overflow lip or standpipeAbout 1–3 cm; depends on flow and lip length. Measure it.
    Media porosityExpanded clay about 40%, lava rock about 45%, gravel about 38% (gravel 25–40%, Freeze & Cherry, 1979)
    NFT film depthA few millimetres (Cooper, 1979); varies with flow, slope and roots
    Sump safety margin20–25% extra free volume. A common design habit, not a published standard.

    For flood-and-drain media beds the FAO manual’s sizing advice, as we could only confirm from secondary summaries, is that the sump should at least be able to hold the water in all the media beds, and be slightly oversized so the pump always has water.

    Limitations and assumptions

    • Straight-sided sump. Free capacity and levels assume vertical walls. Tapered tubs and IBC totes with rounded corners hold a little less per centimetre near the bottom. Use the area at the height of the free space.
    • Worst case at the moment the pump stops. For flood-and-drain beds the calculator assumes every bed is fully flooded when the pump stops. If the beds cycle out of step, less water is up at any one time, but planning for the worst case is safer.
    • Pipes counted full. Gravity drain lines usually run partly full, so their real drain-back is smaller. Pipes that sit below the sump water level do not drain into it and should not be counted.
    • Water absorbed by media and roots, and water clinging to surfaces, drains slowly or not at all. It is ignored, which errs on the safe side.
    • Displacement in the sump. Pumps, heaters and media in the sump take up room. Subtract their volume from the free space, or reduce the usable depth.
    • Test it. The best check is to switch the pump off and watch the sump fill. Do it with someone standing by the plug.
