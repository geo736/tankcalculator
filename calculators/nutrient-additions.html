How the nutrient additions calculator works

A hydroponic recipe is a list of target concentrations: so many mg/L (ppm) of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and so on. Fertilizer salts each carry two or more of those elements in a fixed ratio set by their chemical formula. Potassium nitrate, for example, always brings 13.9% N and 38.7% K. The calculator finds the amount of each salt you ticked that gets the whole solution as close as possible to every target at once.

  1. It takes the target for each element and subtracts what the source water already holds (or, in top-up mode, what the tested solution still holds).
  2. From each salt's formula and IUPAC atomic weights it works out the share of each element in the salt, then scales that by the purity or label analysis you enter.
  3. It solves for the mg/L of each salt with a non-negative least-squares method (Lawson & Hanson, 1974): the amounts can never be negative, and the result minimises the squared relative error over all elements.
  4. It multiplies by the batch volume to give grams, reports what each element ends up at, and estimates the EC of the finished solution with the same ion-sum method as the Nutrient EC calculator.

When the salts you ticked can match the recipe exactly, as with Hoagland's solution and the four salts it was designed from, the fit is exact. When they cannot, for example when your water already holds a lot of calcium, the solver finds the best compromise and the page shows which elements end up over or under target.

Why it matters

Mixing your own nutrients from single salts is cheaper than buying ready-made concentrates and lets you adjust the recipe to your crop and your water. It is also easy to get wrong: the same "200 ppm N" needs very different amounts of salt depending on which salts supply it, and every salt brings a partner ion you may not want. Hard tap or well water can already hold much of the calcium and magnesium a recipe asks for, so ignoring it overdoses those elements and raises EC. In a recirculating system, ions that plants do not take up, such as sodium and chloride, build up over time.

Formulas

Share of an element in a salt, from its formula (n = atoms of the element per formula unit, A = atomic weight, M = molar mass of the salt including any water of crystallisation):

fraction = n × A ÷ M × purity KNO₃: N = 1 × 14.007 ÷ 101.102 = 0.1385 K = 39.098 ÷ 101.102 = 0.3867

Concentration of an element from all salts, with xj the dose of salt j in mg/L and Cwater what is already present:

Ci = Cwater,i + Σj fractionij × xj

The solver chooses x ≥ 0 to minimise the weighted sum of squared relative errors:

minimise Σi [ wi × (Ci − targeti) ÷ max(targeti, floori) ]² + Σ [ 0.3 × added Na or Cl ÷ 100 mg/L ]² w = 1 for NO₃-N, P, K, Ca, Mg and micronutrients; 0.7 for S; 0.5 for NH₄-N floor = 5 mg/L (macronutrients), 0.01 mg/L (micronutrients)

The weights are a design choice, not a standard. They make nitrate, P, K, Ca and Mg fit first, let sulfur run a little over when sulfate salts are needed for magnesium or potassium, and add a small penalty on sodium and chloride, so that a salt such as potassium chloride is used only when nothing else supplies the potassium.

Grams to weigh out, with V in litres:

salt (g) = x (mg/L) × V (L) ÷ 1000 per 100 L = x ÷ 10 per 100 US gal = x × 378.54 ÷ 1000

Fertilizer labels give phosphate and potash as oxides. The conversion factors follow from the atomic weights:

P = P₂O₅ × 2(30.974) ÷ 141.943 = P₂O₅ × 0.4364 K = K₂O × 2(39.098) ÷ 94.195 = K₂O × 0.8301 Ca = CaO × 0.7147 Mg = MgO × 0.6030 S = SO₃ × 0.4005 HCO₃ = alkalinity as CaCO₃ × 1.2193

Why A and B stock tanks

Growers often dissolve the salts as two concentrates, typically 1:100 to 1:200, and dose them into the water. Calcium cannot share a concentrate with sulfate or phosphate: at stock strength calcium sulfate (gypsum) and calcium phosphates exceed their solubility and settle out as a white precipitate that is lost to the plants and can clog drip emitters (Sanchez et al., Penn State Extension). So tank A holds the calcium salts (with nitrates and iron chelate), and tank B holds the phosphates, sulfates and the other micronutrients. At final strength, the same ions stay dissolved. The calculator puts potassium nitrate in tank B; it can go in either.

Each salt also has a limit to how much dissolves in a litre of water, and the limit falls in cold water and when several salts share a tank. Potassium sulfate is the least soluble of the common salts, roughly 110 g per litre of water at 20 °C (CRC Handbook), so the calculator warns when tank B needs more than 100 g/L of it. For the other salts it gives no hard limit: if crystals remain after stirring, use a lower ratio.

Example

The defaults make 100 L of Hoagland & Arnon's solution No. 2 from RO water (nothing in the water) with the four salts Hoagland used: calcium nitrate tetrahydrate, potassium nitrate, monoammonium phosphate and magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, plus micronutrients. Each target fixes one salt:

Ca 160.3 mg/L ÷ 40.078 = 4.000 mmol/L Ca(NO₃)₂·4H₂O × 236.15 = 944.5 mg/L K 234.6 mg/L ÷ 39.098 = 6.000 mmol/L KNO₃ × 101.10 = 606.6 mg/L P 31.0 mg/L ÷ 30.974 = 1.001 mmol/L NH₄H₂PO₄ × 115.03 = 115.1 mg/L Mg 48.6 mg/L ÷ 24.305 = 2.000 mmol/L MgSO₄·7H₂O × 246.47 = 492.8 mg/L

These salts then also supply NO₃-N = (2 × 4.000 + 6.000) × 14.007 = 196.1 mg/L, NH₄-N = 14.0 mg/L and S = 64.1 mg/L, exactly the remaining targets. For 100 L that is 94.45 g calcium nitrate, 60.66 g potassium nitrate, 11.51 g MAP and 49.28 g magnesium sulfate (357.5, 229.6, 43.6 and 186.6 g per 100 US gallons). The micronutrients add 0.909 g Fe-DTPA (1 mg/L Fe at 11%), 0.286 g boric acid, 0.180 g manganese chloride, 0.022 g zinc sulfate, 0.0079 g copper sulfate and 0.0025 g sodium molybdate: the same amounts as Hoagland's micronutrient stock. In total, 217.3 g of product.

The estimated EC is 2.30 mS/cm, the same as the Nutrient EC calculator gives for this recipe: the manganese chloride adds 0.65 mg/L chloride and the molybdate 0.005 mg/L sodium. At 1:100 each 10 L stock tank holds 94.45 g/L calcium nitrate and 0.909 g/L Fe-DTPA in A, and 60.66 g/L KNO₃, 11.51 g/L MAP and 49.28 g/L MgSO₄·7H₂O plus micronutrients in B, enough for 1,000 L.

Source water and top-ups

Enter your water report in mg/L. Calcium, magnesium, sulfate and nitrate in the water count towards the targets. Sodium, chloride and bicarbonate count towards EC. Bicarbonate is not removed by fertilizer salts: water with high alkalinity drives the pH up, so neutralise it with acid (see the pH Down calculator). Nitric, phosphoric or sulfuric acid then add nitrogen, phosphorus or sulfur; you can enter those amounts as part of the water analysis so the calculator adds less fertilizer. If the pH is too low, the pH Up calculator works out a potassium or calcium base.

In top-up mode, fill the tank back to the batch volume with water, mix, and enter a lab analysis of the solution. The calculator then works out only what is missing. Plants take up elements at different rates, so an EC reading alone cannot tell you which ones are short. Elements that are already above target cannot be removed by adding salts; only dilution or a fresh batch lowers them.

Limitations and assumptions

  • Purity and hydrates. The salt masses assume the formula shown. Magnesium sulfate sold as anhydrous or monohydrate, or calcium nitrate sold as the commercial double salt, has a different element content; change the purity, or use the commercial grade or custom fertilizer rows with your label analysis.
  • Ion interactions. The calculation is a mass balance. It does not model ion pairs, precipitation at final strength, or uptake by plants. The EC estimate is the Nutrient EC method: Kohlrausch sum × 0.90, a rule of thumb that can be off by 10% or more for recipes high in sulfate or magnesium (Fernández, 2020).
  • pH effects. Ammonium and phosphate salts lower pH and nitrate uptake raises it. The phosphate is counted as H₂PO₄⁻, the main form between pH 5.5 and 6.5. Check pH after mixing.
  • Micronutrients. Each micronutrient comes from its own salt, so these normally match exactly. Iron chelates, and chelated micronutrient mixes, also contain sodium or ammonium that is not counted.
  • The ±10% band used to flag elements as over or under target is a display rule of thumb, not a crop tolerance.
  • Recipes. Hoagland & Arnon's solutions were designed for research on many plant species. Commercial crops are often grown on recipes tuned to the crop, stage and climate; enter those as custom targets.

Safety

  • Wear gloves and eye protection when weighing and dissolving salts and handling concentrates. Store them dry, labelled and out of reach of children and animals.
  • Ammonium nitrate and the other nitrates are oxidisers: keep them away from fuels, organic matter and heat, and check local rules for ammonium nitrate.
  • Dissolve each salt in water before adding the next; never pour the A and B concentrates into each other.
  • Nutrient solution is not drinking water. Dispose of spent solution according to local rules; it is a fertilizer rich in nitrate and phosphate.
