How the iron addition calculator works

The calculator works out how many grams of iron (Fe) must go into the water to lift it from the current level to the target level. It then divides by the share of iron in your product to get the amount of product to weigh out (powder) or measure (liquid). Every input has its own unit, so you can enter the system volume in US gallons and the iron level in mg/L, for example. The calculator also checks your system pH against the range in which each type of iron chelate is known to hold its iron.

Why iron matters in aquaponics and hydroponics

Plants need iron to make chlorophyll. When it runs short, the youngest leaves turn pale yellow between green veins (interveinal chlorosis). Fish feed supplies little iron, so aquaponic systems are commonly iron deficient and need regular iron supplements, especially in the first months (Somerville et al., 2014; Kasozi et al., 2019). In hydroponics, iron is part of the nutrient recipe, but it can drop out of solution when the pH rises.

Iron is added in a chelated form. A chelate is an organic molecule that wraps around the iron ion and keeps it dissolved. Without one, iron reacts with oxygen and hydroxide in neutral water and settles out as rust-coloured ferric hydroxide that plants cannot use.

Formulas

Iron needed, with volume V in litres and concentrations in mg/L:

Fe added (mg) = (Ctarget − Ccurrent) × V

Powder or granules, with the label iron content as a mass fraction (11% = 0.11):

Product (g) = Fe added (g) ÷ fraction Fe

Liquid, with the label strength in % w/v (1% w/v = 1 g per 100 mL = 10 g/L):

Product (mL) = Fe added (g) ÷ (strength % w/v ÷ 100)

Dose rates: product per 100 L = product ÷ V × 100, and per 100 US gallons = product ÷ V × 378.54 (1 US gal = 3.785411784 L exactly).

Which chelate at which pH

As pH rises, hydroxide and calcium compete with the chelate for the iron. Equilibrium calculations (Norvell, 1972; Lindsay, 1979) and growth trials show the practical upper limits in the table: Fe-EDTA up to about pH 6.5, Fe-DTPA up to about pH 7.5, and Fe-EDDHA up to about pH 9 (Hershkowitz et al., 2025). These limits are approximate and different sources quote slightly different cut-offs, so the calculator shows “near limit” within 0.3 pH units of each one. Aquaponic systems are usually run at pH 6–7 as a compromise between fish, plants and nitrifying bacteria (Somerville et al., 2014), which suits Fe-DTPA. Fe-EDDHA is the safer choice when pH regularly goes above 7.5, but it costs more and tints the water red.

Ferrous sulfate is not a chelate. Dissolved ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) is oxidised by dissolved oxygen, and the rate rises about 100-fold for every 1-unit rise in pH (Stumm & Lee, 1961). In well-aerated water near pH 7 it turns to insoluble ferric hydroxide within minutes to hours. The calculator flags ferrous sulfate as unsuitable above pH 6.0. That cut-off is a rule of thumb, not a sharp limit. Most of the iron will precipitate in an aquaponic system, and it can coat surfaces and fish gills.

Target iron levels (rules of thumb)

  • Aquaponics: about 1–2 mg/L Fe. The FAO manual suggests chelated iron at about this level, especially in new systems (Somerville et al., 2014). The University of the Virgin Islands system added chelated iron at 2 mg/L every three weeks (Rakocy et al., 2004). The calculator default is 2 mg/L.
  • Hydroponics: nutrient recipes commonly contain about 1–3 mg/L Fe. This is a rule of thumb; follow the recipe for your crop.

These are guidance ranges, not hard limits. Plants keep taking up iron, so the level falls between doses. Test again before each top-up.

Example

A 1,000 L aquaponic system has no measurable iron (0 mg/L), and the target is 2 mg/L. The iron needed is (2 − 0) × 1,000 = 2,000 mg = 2 g of Fe. Using Fe-DTPA powder with 11% Fe: 2 ÷ 0.11 = 18.2 g of product. That is 1.82 g per 100 L, or 18.18 ÷ 1,000 × 378.54 = 6.88 g per 100 US gallons. At pH 7.0 Fe-DTPA is within its stable range (up to about 7.5), so the check passes.

Things the calculator does not include

  • Test kit accuracy. Hobby iron test kits are coarse at low levels. A reading of “0” may still mean a little iron is present.
  • Uptake and losses. Plants, biofilm and solids filters remove iron over time. The result is a single top-up, not a weekly need.
  • Liquid labels in % w/w. Many liquid products state iron as a percentage by weight. Convert to w/v by multiplying by the product’s specific gravity (for example, 6% w/w × 1.25 g/mL = 7.5% w/v).
  • EDDHA isomers. Only the ortho-ortho form of Fe-EDDHA is strongly stable at high pH. Products differ in how much of their iron is in that form.
  • Mixing. Dissolve the product in a bucket of system water and add it slowly where water is moving, not straight onto fish.
