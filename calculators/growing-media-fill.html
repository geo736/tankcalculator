How the growing media calculator works

The calculator finds the volume of media that fits in one bed up to the fill depth you enter, multiplies it by the number of beds, and adds the extra percentage you choose for settling. That is the volume to buy. Dividing by the bag size and rounding up gives the number of bags. Every dimension has its own unit, so you can enter the bed in feet and the bag in liters.

Two properties of the media then give the weights:

  • Bulk density is the dry weight of a loose-filled volume of media, air gaps included (for example kg per m³ or lb per ft³). It gives the dry weight you have to carry.
  • Water-filled pore space is the share of the bed volume that water can fill when the bed is flooded to the top of the media. It gives the water held in the bed and, added to the dry weight, the saturated weight.

Why it matters for aquaponics and hydroponics

In a media-bed aquaponic unit the media is more than plant support. It is also the mechanical filter that traps fish solids and the surface where nitrifying bacteria live. The FAO aquaponics manual (Somerville et al., 2014) describes a media bed about 30 cm (12 in) deep as having three layers: a dry zone at the top, a root zone where water floods and drains, and a solids collection and mineralization zone at the bottom. Kasozi et al. (2020) found different bacterial communities in each of these zones. The manual recommends about 30 cm for large fruiting crops such as tomatoes and 15–20 cm for small leafy greens. The SRAC fact sheet on small-scale aquaponics notes that most media beds are 6–12 in (15–30 cm) deep. These are rules of thumb, and the calculator shows which band your fill depth falls in.

Media is heavy. A bed full of wet gravel can weigh several hundred kilograms per square meter, so the saturated weight and floor load help you size a stand, check a deck or balcony, or choose a lighter medium. The water held in the media also matters: when a flood-and-drain bed fills, that water leaves the fish tank or sump, and the sump must be large enough to take it back when the bed drains.

Formulas

Bed volume for one bed, with fill depth d (limited to the wall height):

Rectangular bed: Vbed = L × W × d Round container: Vbed = π × (D ÷ 2)² × d

Volume to buy and bags for n beds with an extra settling allowance e (%):

Vbuy = Vbed × n × (1 + e ÷ 100) Bags = ⌈ Vbuy ÷ Vbag ⌉ (rounded up) Cost = Bags × price per bag

Weights, with bulk density ρb, water-filled pore space φ and water density ρw = 0.998 kg/L (freshwater at 20 °C):

Dry weight = Vbuy × ρb Water held = φ × Vbed × n Saturated mass = Dry weight + Water held × ρw Floor load = Saturated mass of one bed ÷ bed footprint area

The dry weight uses the volume you buy, because the extra media settles into the same bed. The water uses the installed bed volume, because that is the space that floods.

Example

One rectangular bed, 120 cm long, 60 cm wide and 35 cm high, filled with expanded clay to 30 cm. Bed volume = 1.2 × 0.6 × 0.3 = 0.216 m³ = 216 L. With 5% extra for settling you need 216 × 1.05 = 226.8 L. In 50 L bags that is 226.8 ÷ 50 = 4.54, rounded up to 5 bags, which at 30 per bag cost 150.

At 350 kg/m³ (0.35 kg/L) the dry media weighs 226.8 × 0.35 = 79.4 kg. Flooded, it holds 0.40 × 216 = 86.4 L of water (86.2 kg). The saturated bed weighs 79.4 + 86.2 = 165.6 kg, spread over 1.2 × 0.6 = 0.72 m², or about 230 kg/m² (47 lb/ft²), before the weight of the bed itself, the plants and any standing water.

Things the calculator does not include

  • The container. Add the weight of the bed, the stand, the plumbing and the plants.
  • Water absorbed into the particles. Expanded clay, lava rock and perlite also soak up some water inside each particle over time, which adds weight. Coir and rockwool hold water after draining. Use a higher pore space value for a safety margin.
  • Bell siphons, standpipes and media guards. These take up a small amount of space in the bed. Subtract their volume if they are large.
  • Media that floats. Perlite floats when a bed floods, and some light expanded clay floats until it is soaked. Perlite, coir and rockwool are hydroponic substrates; for aquaponic flood-and-drain beds, expanded clay, lava rock or non-calcareous gravel are the usual choices. Limestone or marble gravel slowly raises pH and is best avoided (Somerville et al., 2014).
  • Compressed coir. Coir bricks expand when soaked. Enter the expanded volume per brick from the label as the bag size.
