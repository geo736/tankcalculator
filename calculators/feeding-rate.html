How the feeding rate calculator works

Fish farmers usually set the daily ration as a percentage of the fish's body weight (% BW per day). The calculator first finds the total weight of fish in the tank, the biomass, either from the number of fish times their average weight or from a biomass you enter directly. It multiplies the biomass by the feeding rate to get the daily feed, then divides that by the number of feedings. Weekly and monthly totals assume the same daily ration every day.

If you enter a feed conversion ratio (FCR), the calculator also estimates growth. FCR is the weight of feed given divided by the weight the fish gain, so an FCR of 1.5 means 1.5 kg of feed produces 1 kg of fish.

Why feeding rate matters

Feed is usually the largest running cost in fish farming, and it is also the input that drives the whole system. In aquaponics the fish feed is the nutrient source for the plants, so the daily ration sets how much growing area the system can support. In recirculating aquaculture (RAS) the daily feed sets how much ammonia the biofilter must remove, how much oxygen the fish use and how much solid waste the filters must catch (Timmons & Ebeling, 2010). Underfeeding slows growth; overfeeding wastes money and leaves uneaten feed that rots and pollutes the water.

Formulas

Biomass B = number of fish × average weight Daily feed F = B × rate (%) ÷ 100 Feed per feeding = F ÷ feedings per day Weekly feed = F × 7 Monthly feed = F × 30

With a feed conversion ratio:

Biomass gain per day = F ÷ FCR Gain per 30 days = 30 × F ÷ FCR Weight per fish, 30 days = average weight + (30 × F ÷ FCR) ÷ number of fish

Aquaponic system balance (FAO feed rate ratio for leafy greens, 40–50 g feed per m² of growing area per day):

Growing area = F (g/day) ÷ 50 to F (g/day) ÷ 40 m²

Feeding rate by fish size (general guidance)

Small fish eat a much larger share of their body weight than large fish. The table below is the size-based daily feeding guide for tilapia in recirculating systems at 28 °C (82 °F), from Riche & Garling (2003), who adapted it from the National Research Council (1993). The FAO tilapia feeding table for intensive farming gives matching ranges for fish from 1 g to 100 g and splits the over-100 g class into 3–2% and 2–1.5%. Within each class, use the higher rate for fish at the small end and the lower rate as they approach the next class.

Fish weightFeeding rate (% body weight per day)
0–1 g (fry)30–10%
1–5 g10–6%
5–20 g (fingerlings)6–4%
20–100 g (juveniles)4–3%
Over 100 g (grow-out)3–1.5%

The FAO aquaponics manual (Somerville et al., 2014) gives a simpler rule of thumb for the common aquaponic species: fish larger than about 50 g eat 1–2% of their body weight per day during grow-out, and newly stocked juveniles of about 50 g can be fed about 3% per day. The chart shows this 1–2% band as a dashed outline.

These figures are a starting point, not a fixed rule. Appetite falls sharply in water cooler than the species' preferred range, and it also changes with feed energy and protein, water quality, oxygen and stress. Cold-water species such as trout and warm-water species such as catfish have their own feeding charts, which feed manufacturers usually print on the bag. Watch the fish: a common practice is to feed only what they clear in a few minutes and remove uneaten feed.

Feedings per day

Fry and fingerlings grow fast and have small stomachs, so they do best with many small meals; Riche & Garling (2003) recommend feeding fingerlings at least four times a day. Larger fish are usually fed two or three times a day. Splitting the ration does not change the daily total, but smaller meals are eaten more completely and spread the ammonia and oxygen load more evenly through the day.

Feed conversion ratio

FCR depends on species, size, feed quality, temperature and how carefully the fish are fed. Well-fed tilapia on a good commercial pellet commonly convert at roughly 1.2–2 kg of feed per kg of gain, and 1.5 is a common planning figure. Treat this as a rule of thumb and replace it with your own measurement: weigh the fish at the start and end of a period and divide the feed used by the weight gained.

Example

A tank holds 100 tilapia averaging 250 g, so the biomass is 100 × 250 g = 25 kg. These fish are in the grow-out class (3–1.5%), so feed them 2% of body weight per day: 25 kg × 0.02 = 0.5 kg (500 g) of feed per day. Split into 3 feedings, each meal is 500 ÷ 3 ≈ 166.7 g. That is 3.5 kg per week and 15 kg per 30 days.

With an FCR of 1.5 the fish should gain 500 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 333 g of biomass per day, or 10 kg over 30 days. Shared between 100 fish that is 100 g each, so the average fish would grow from 250 g to about 350 g in a month, if the ration stays the same and no fish are lost. In an aquaponic system, 500 g of feed per day can support about 500 ÷ 50 = 10 m² to 500 ÷ 40 = 12.5 m² of leafy greens.

Things the calculator does not include

  • Growth during the period. Fish get heavier every day, so a fixed ration becomes a smaller percentage of body weight. Re-weigh a sample every one to two weeks and recalculate. The 30-day weight estimate uses a constant daily ration.
  • Mortality. Count losses and remove them from the fish number.
  • Temperature. The size table is for tilapia at 28 °C. Feed less in cooler water and follow a species-specific chart for other fish.
  • Plant balance. The leafy-green area uses the FAO ratio for a 32% protein feed. Fruiting crops need more feed per m² (FAO gives 50–80 g/m²/day).
