How the internal tank velocity calculator works

In a circular culture tank the water enters through one or more inlet pipes with small ports or nozzles aimed along the wall. The jets push the whole body of water around the tank, so it rotates like a slow whirlpool. The speed of that rotation is the internal tank velocity (also called rotational or circulation velocity).

The calculator first finds the inlet jet velocity: the total flow divided by the total open area of all the ports. It then estimates the mean rotational velocity with the design rule of thumb from Timmons, Summerfelt & Vinci (1998): the rotational velocity is roughly 15–20% of the inlet velocity. Because this ratio is a rule of thumb and depends on the inlet design, the result is shown as a range, not a single number. The middle of the range (17.5%) is used for the drawing.

Why tank velocity matters

  • Self-cleaning. The rotating water sweeps uneaten feed and faeces across the floor to the center drain. Timmons et al. (1998) report that velocities greater than about 15–30 cm/s are needed to move settleable solids to the drain.
  • Fish exercise and welfare. Water velocities of 0.5–2.0 body lengths per second are considered best for fish health, muscle tone and respiration (Losordo & Westers, 1994, cited in Timmons et al., 1998). Faster water makes fish spend energy that would otherwise go into growth; very small fish may not be able to hold position at all.
  • Mixing. A well-rotating tank has fairly uniform water quality from wall to center, so oxygen and waste do not collect in dead zones.

These goals can conflict: a velocity high enough to self-clean may be too fast for small fingerlings. That is why many farms use inlet pipes whose ports can be swapped or rotated, and adjust the velocity as the fish grow (Davidson & Summerfelt, 2004).

Formulas

Tank volume and retention time (D = diameter, d = water depth, Q = total inflow):

V = π × (D / 2)² × d HRT = V / Q Exchanges per hour = 3600 s / HRT (in seconds)

Inlet jet velocity with n ports of diameter dp:

Aports = n × π × dp² / 4 vin = Q / Aports

Estimated rotational velocity (rule of thumb, k ≈ 0.15–0.20):

vtank ≈ k × vin, k = 0.15 … 0.20

Fish swimming speed in body lengths per second (L = fish length):

BL/s = vtank / L

Jet and port size for a target velocity, rearranging the same relation:

vin, needed = vtarget / k dp, needed = √( 4 × Q / (n × π × vin, needed) )

Velocity head of the jet, the least extra pressure head the inlet pipe must supply to make jets this fast (friction and nozzle losses come on top, so the real head is higher):

h = vin² / (2 g), g = 9.80665 m/s²

Where the 15–20% comes from

Tvinnereim & Skybakmoen (1989) showed that the rotation in a circular tank is driven by the momentum (impulse force) of the inlet jets, F = ρ Q (vin − vtank), balanced against friction at the walls, floor and fish. Doubling the jet velocity at the same flow therefore increases the rotation, but not in exact proportion for every tank. For design, Timmons et al. (1998) summarize the rotational velocity as roughly proportional to the velocity through the inlet openings, with a constant of about 0.15–0.20 (citing Paul et al., 1991). The real constant depends on the nozzle layout, tank roughness, depth and fish load, so treat the range as a first estimate and measure the velocity in your own tank with a flow meter or a floating object.

Example

A 4 m diameter tank holds water 0.8 m deep: V = π × 2² × 0.8 = 10.05 m³ (10,053 L). With 400 L/min (6.667 L/s) of inflow, the retention time is 10,053 ÷ 6.667 = 1,508 s = 25.1 min, or 2.39 exchanges per hour, inside the 20–32 min range used by Davidson & Summerfelt (2004).

The water enters through 8 ports of 25 mm. Total port area = 8 × π × 0.025² ÷ 4 = 0.003927 m², so the jets leave at 0.006667 ÷ 0.003927 = 1.70 m/s. The estimated rotation is 0.15–0.20 × 1.70 = 25.5–34.0 cm/s. For 20 cm fish that is 1.27–1.70 body lengths per second, within the 0.5–2.0 BL/s guidance, and the velocity is in or above the 15–30 cm/s band needed to carry solids to the drain.

To aim for 30 cm/s you need jets of 30 ÷ 0.20 = 1.50 m/s to 30 ÷ 0.15 = 2.00 m/s. With the same flow and 8 ports, that means ports of about 23.0–26.6 mm.

Limitations and assumptions

  • The 15–20% ratio is a rule of thumb. It is not a fitted equation for your tank. Real tanks can be faster or slower, especially with unusual nozzle angles, very deep tanks, baffles or heavy fish loads.
  • Velocity is not uniform. The water is usually fastest near the wall and slowest near the center. The calculator gives a single mean value.
  • Jets must be aimed along the wall. The estimate assumes ports point roughly tangentially in the same direction. Ports aimed at the center or against each other produce little rotation.
  • Swimming ability depends on species, size and temperature. 0.5–2.0 BL/s is a general guide; check species-specific data for sensitive or very small fish.
  • Self-cleaning also needs drain flow. Rotation moves solids to the center, but the center drain must take enough flow to remove them. Davidson & Summerfelt (2004) found a bottom-center drain flow of at least 5–6 L/min per m² of tank floor flushed settleable solids quickly.
  • Geometry. Timmons et al. (1998) recommend diameter-to-depth ratios of about 5:1 to 10:1 for good rotation and self-cleaning; the result list shows your ratio against this range.
