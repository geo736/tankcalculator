How the hydrostatic pressure calculator works

Still water presses on everything it touches, and the push gets stronger the deeper you go because every layer of water carries the weight of the water above it. The pressure at a point depends only on the depth below the surface and the density of the water. It does not depend on the size or shape of the tank: a narrow standpipe and a large pond of the same depth have the same pressure at the bottom.

The calculator converts each input from its own unit to SI units, finds the pressure at the floor and at your chosen point, and then works out the force on one flat, vertical wall. On a wall the pressure is zero at the surface and grows in a straight line to its maximum at the floor, so the pressure diagram is a triangle (shown on the right of the drawing). The total force is the area of that triangle times the wall width, and it acts at the triangle's centroid, one third of the depth up from the floor.

Why it matters in aquaculture, aquaponics and hydroponics

  • Tank walls and liners. The force on a wall grows with the square of the depth. Doubling the depth from 2 ft to 4 ft quadruples the push on each wall. This is why deep tanks need thicker panels, ribs, cages (as on IBC totes) or ties across the top, and why bulges and leaks usually show up near the bottom.
  • Round tanks. In a round tank with a flexible liner or a thin wall, the wall is held in tension. The ring (hoop) tension per unit height at depth z is T = p × r, where r is the tank radius, so it is largest at the bottom and grows with tank diameter.
  • Valves, fittings and sensors. The static pressure at a bottom drain, valve or pressure-type level sensor is the value at its depth. Static head in culture tanks is small (about 0.43 psi per foot of fresh water), far below the rating of normal PVC fittings, but bulkhead seals, sight glasses and low-range pressure transducers must still suit it.
  • Aeration. An air diffuser at depth only releases air when the blower supplies more than the water pressure at the diffuser, plus the losses in pipes and the diffuser itself (Timmons & Ebeling, 2010). Enter the diffuser depth as the point depth to see that minimum.
  • Gravity-fed flow. A head tank that feeds grow beds or a pipe outlet below the tank delivers water because of this pressure difference. The pressure at an outlet below the floor of a tank is found from its depth below the water surface, which is why the point depth may be larger than the water depth.

Formulas

Gauge pressure (pressure above the atmosphere) at depth z below the surface:

p = ρ × g × z

where ρ is the water density (kg/m³), g = 9.80665 m/s² is standard gravity and z is in meters, giving p in pascals (Pa = N/m²). The tank floor is at z = h, the water depth, and is loaded uniformly with p = ρ g h.

Absolute pressure adds the air pressure pressing on the water surface. The calculator uses one standard atmosphere:

pabs = p + 101.325 kPa

Force on a flat vertical wall of width w that spans the full water depth h:

Average pressure pavg = ½ ρ g h Force per width F/w = ½ ρ g h² Total force F = ½ ρ g h² w Center of pressure h/3 above the floor (2h/3 below the surface)

The “conventional” water-column units assume water of exactly 1,000 kg/m³ under standard gravity. Real freshwater at 20 °C (998 kg/m³) is 0.2% lighter, so 1 m of it gives 0.998 m H₂O, not exactly 1. Seawater (1025 kg/m³) gives 2.5% more than its depth.

Example

A tank holds 4 ft (1.2192 m) of freshwater at 20 °C. One flat wall is 8 ft (2.4384 m) wide.

p = 998 × 9.80665 × 1.2192 = 11,932 Pa = 11.93 kPa = 1.73 psi pabs = 11.93 + 101.325 = 113.26 kPa = 16.43 psi F/w = ½ × 998 × 9.80665 × 1.2192² = 7,274 N/m (498 lbf/ft) F = 7,274 × 2.4384 = 17,737 N ≈ 3,987 lbf Center of pressure = 4 ft ÷ 3 = 1.33 ft above the floor

The floor carries 11,932 Pa, which is 249.2 lb on every square foot (1,217 kg per m²). At a valve 2 ft below the surface the gauge pressure is half the bottom value, 0.865 psi.

Assumptions and limits

  • Still water. Results are static pressures. Pump surges, water hammer from fast-closing valves, sloshing, waves and earthquakes add load and are not included.
  • Density. Water density changes slightly with temperature and salinity. Between 0 and 30 °C freshwater stays within about 0.3% of 998 kg/m³ (USGS), so the effect on pressure is small. Use the custom option for brackish water or strong nutrient brines.
  • Wall shape. The force formula is for a flat, vertical, rectangular wall that runs from the surface to the floor with the water side open to air. Sloped walls, curved walls and walls with a ledge need the general plane-surface method in a fluid mechanics text.
  • Atmospheric pressure. Absolute pressure uses the sea-level standard atmosphere. Air pressure is lower at altitude and changes with the weather; gauge pressure and wall force are not affected.
  • Not a structural design. The force is the load from the water only. Choosing wall thickness, bracing and fasteners needs material strengths and safety factors; ask an engineer or the tank maker for large or tall tanks.
