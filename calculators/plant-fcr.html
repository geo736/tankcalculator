How the plant FCR calculator works

Fish farmers judge feeding with the feed conversion ratio (FCR): kilograms of feed given per kilogram of fish weight gained. In aquaponics the same feed also fertilizes the plants, so Colt and Semmens (2022) proposed an FCR for the plants as well: the plant feed conversion ratio, FCRplant = feed supplied ÷ increase in plant weight [1]. It tells you how well the nutrients in the fish feed end up as plant tissue. A lower FCRplant means more plant growth from each kilogram of feed.

In Measure mode you enter the feed given to the fish during a growing period and the plant weight gained in the same period (harvest minus transplants). The page divides one by the other. If you add the grow area and the period, it also gives the feed loading rate in g of feed per m² of grow area per day and compares it with the guideline ranges used on the Feed Rate to Grow Space calculator. If you weighed the fish, it adds the ordinary fish FCR, the total food produced per kilogram of feed and the plant-fish mass ratio.

In Plan mode you enter a target plant production and an FCRplant (ideally one you measured). The page multiplies them to get the feed you will need, and with a period and an area turns that into feed per day and a loading rate.

Why it matters

Feed is the main running cost and the only real nutrient input in most aquaponic systems. The usual sizing rule, the feed rate ratio, says how much feed a square metre of plants can use (for example 40–50 g/m²/day for leafy greens) [3, 4], but it does not tell you how much the plants actually grew from it. FCRplant closes that loop: a poor (high) FCRplant may point to excessive loss of nutrients, poor water quality or poor growing conditions [1]. Colt and Semmens strongly recommend that FCRplant be reported in aquaponic research, and note that a farmer can use it for routine management with data that is normally collected anyway [1]. A 2026 study of Bibb lettuce with Nile tilapia used FCRplant and the plant-fish mass ratio to judge six feed loading rates (15–90 g/m²/day of a 32% protein feed) and reported that 30, 45, 60 and 75 g/m²/day were within standard operating performance for protein and energy retention, while 15 and 90 were not [2].

Following [1], all weights are wet weights, and the plant weight is the salable product only: roots, stems and trimmings are not counted, and the starting weight of fruiting crops such as tomato, cucumber and pepper is zero. The inverse, kg of plant per kg of feed, is the plant feed conversion efficiency (FCEplant), which can be easier to read because FCRplant is often well below 1 [1].

The theoretical FCRplant

Unlike the fish FCR, the plant FCR has a theoretical value that can be worked out from a mass balance [1]. Nitrogen in the feed is FF × DMfeed × Nfeed; a fraction NUEplant of it ends up in the salable plant, which contains ΔWplant × DMplant × Nplant of nitrogen. Setting the two equal and rearranging gives the FCRplant that nitrogen supports, and the same for phosphorus. Whichever element needs more feed per kg of plant is the limiting one, and its FCRplant is the controlling value. Make-up water is assumed to bring no N or P.

Using mean feed compositions and the selected mean retention values for aquaponic plants (22.3% of feed nitrogen and 15.1% of feed phosphorus), Colt and Semmens found theoretical FCRplant values of 0.064 to 0.564 kg feed/kg plant across 12 vegetables and herbs with warm- or cold-water feed. Phosphorus was limiting for lettuce, tomato, cucumber, pak choi, Swiss chard and jalapeño with warm-water feed, and nitrogen for spinach, basil, parsley, kale, peppermint and mustard greens [1].

Only about 51% of the experimental FCRplant values they could calculate from 74 published aquaponic studies fell inside that theoretical range; many were well above it, some above 2 [1]. They read this as substandard performance of the plant part of many systems, which may come from (a) low nutrient and micronutrient concentrations, (b) suboptimal pH and temperatures, or (c) a build-up of salts [1]. This page compares your measured value with the theoretical one for your crop and feed. A ratio near 1 means your plants retain feed nutrients about as well as the paper's average; well above 1 means more feed per kg of plants than the mass balance predicts. A value below the theory is possible when the water, supplements or mineralised solids supply extra N or P, or when your plants retain more than the average.

Formulas

Theoretical FCRplant from the nitrogen and phosphorus balances (Eqs. 16 and 18 in [1]; all compositions as decimal fractions, plant N and P on a dry basis):

FCRN = (DMplant ÷ DMfeed) × (Nplant ÷ Nfeed) ÷ NUEplant FCRP = (DMplant ÷ DMfeed) × (Pplant ÷ Pfeed) ÷ PUEplant FCRcontrol = the larger of FCRN and FCRP

Measured values, with F = feed supplied over the period, H = wet weight of salable harvest, S = its weight at the start, d = dry matter (%), T = period (days), A = grow area (m²), Gfish = fish weight gain:

Gplant = H − S FCRplant = F ÷ Gplant (kg feed per kg plant, fresh) Plant per kg feed = Gplant ÷ F = 1 ÷ FCRplant FCRplant, dry = F ÷ (Gplant × d ÷ 100) Loading rate = F (g) ÷ A ÷ T (g/m²/day) Yield = Gplant ÷ A FCRfish = F ÷ Gfish Food per kg feed = (Gplant + Gfish) ÷ F PFRM = Gplant ÷ Gfish = FCRfish ÷ FCRplant

Plan mode turns the measured formula around: feed needed = target plant gain × FCRplant, feed per day = feed ÷ T, and the grow area that feed fits at the guideline ratio R is feed per day ÷ R. The last line, PFRM = FCRfish ÷ FCRplant, is Eq. 9 of [1]: both FCRs use the same feed, so dividing them cancels it.

Example

Theory first. Lettuce is 4.37% dry matter with 4.94% N and 0.76% P in the dry matter; the mean warm-water feed is 91.5% dry matter with 5.21% N and 1.02% P [1]. Nitrogen: (4.37 ÷ 91.5) × (4.94 ÷ 5.21) ÷ 0.223 = 0.203. Phosphorus: (4.37 ÷ 91.5) × (0.76 ÷ 1.02) ÷ 0.151 = 0.236. Phosphorus needs more feed, so it is limiting and the theoretical FCRplant is 0.236 kg feed per kg lettuce (about 4.2 kg of lettuce per kg of feed), as in Table 3 of [1].

Now a measurement. A 10 m² lettuce raft is fed from a fish tank for 35 days. The fish get 15.75 kg of feed in that time (450 g a day, which is 45 g/m²/day). You planted 1 kg of seedlings and harvested 36 kg of salable lettuce, so the plants gained 35 kg. FCRplant = 15.75 ÷ 35 = 0.45 kg feed per kg lettuce, or 35 ÷ 15.75 = 2.22 kg of lettuce per kg of feed. That is 0.45 ÷ 0.236 = 1.91 times the theoretical value: inside the paper's 0.064–0.564 range, but the plants turned feed nutrients into lettuce only about half as well as the mass balance with average retention predicts. At 4.37% dry matter the dry gain is 35 × 0.0437 = 1.53 kg, so the dry-weight FCRplant is 15.75 ÷ 1.53 ≈ 10.3. The plants gained 35 ÷ 10 = 3.5 kg/m². The loading rate is 15 750 g ÷ 10 m² ÷ 35 days = 45 g/m²/day, inside the FAO leafy-green range of 40–50. The fish gained 10.5 kg, so their FCR is 15.75 ÷ 10.5 = 1.5, the system made (35 + 10.5) ÷ 15.75 = 2.89 kg of food per kg of feed, and the plant-fish mass ratio is 35 ÷ 10.5 = 3.33 (= 1.5 ÷ 0.45).

Plan mode turns this around. To grow 35 kg of lettuce in 35 days at an FCRplant of 0.45 you need 35 × 0.45 = 15.75 kg of feed, or 450 g/day. At 40–50 g/m²/day that feed fits 450 ÷ 50 = 9.0 to 450 ÷ 40 = 11.25 m² of grow area; on 10 m² the loading rate is 45 g/m²/day and the plants give 3.5 kg/m². At a fish FCR of 1.5 the fish gain 15.75 ÷ 1.5 = 10.5 kg.

Limitations and assumptions

  • Same period, same feed. Feed, plant gain and fish gain must cover the same days. Count all plants harvested in the period and the change in standing crop if beds were not empty at both ends.
  • Wet weight varies. Wet weight depends on water content and trimming. Weigh the salable product consistently; dry weight is a fairer basis for comparing crops.
  • The theory uses averages. The theoretical value depends strongly on the retention values (NUE and PUE), which vary widely between studies; [1] used averages over the studies it selected, and found unaccounted losses of 30–50% of nitrogen and 10–20% of phosphorus inputs. Crop composition also depends on variety and growing conditions. Enter your own values if you have them.
  • Only N and P. The mass balance ignores N and P in the make-up water, and potassium, calcium, iron or other elements can limit growth before N or P do [1].
  • Supplements. Iron, potassium or calcium added to the water also feed the plants but are not counted in F [3, 4].
  • Solids removal. Systems that remove fish solids lose part of the feed nutrients; systems with mineralization recover more. Both change FCRplant.
  • Loading-rate ranges are rules of thumb for warm-water fish on a ~32% protein feed [2, 3, 4].
