How the feed rate ratio calculator works

In aquaponics the fish feed is the fertilizer. Fish eat the feed, excrete ammonia and solids, bacteria turn the ammonia into nitrate, and the plants take up the nitrate and other nutrients. Because almost every nutrient enters the system through the feed, the simplest way to balance fish and plants is to match the amount of feed added per day to the area of plants that will use it. That number, grams of feed per square metre of growing area per day, is called the feed rate ratio (Rakocy et al. call it the feeding rate ratio) [1, 2].

The calculator works in two directions:

  • Daily feed → area. Divide the daily feed by the ratio. The high end of the ratio gives the smallest area, and the low end gives the largest area. If you also enter your actual grow area, the page tells you whether it is inside, under or over that range.
  • Grow area → feed. Multiply the area by the ratio to get the range of daily feed the plants can use. If you also enter the feed you give now, the page checks it against that range.

In both modes the daily feed is turned into an approximate fish biomass by dividing by the feeding rate as a percentage of body weight.

Why it matters

Too little growing area for the feed means nutrients (and nitrate) build up faster than the plants remove them, and water quality for the fish gets worse. Too much growing area for the feed means the plants run short of nutrients and grow slowly or show deficiencies. Using the ratio at the design stage lets you size the fish tank, the stocking and the beds together instead of guessing. The FAO manual uses this ratio as its main method to size a small-scale unit [1], and the University of the Virgin Islands (UVI) raft system was designed with it [2].

Guideline ratios

Crop or systemFeed rate ratioSource
Leafy greens (lettuce, herbs, chard…)40–50 g/m²/daySomerville et al. 2014 [1]
Fruiting vegetables (tomato, pepper, cucumber…)50–80 g/m²/daySomerville et al. 2014 [1]
Raft (deep water culture) hydroponics, UVI system60–100 g/m²/dayRakocy, Masser & Losordo 2006 [2]

These ranges are rules of thumb from practical experience. The FAO figures assume a standard fish feed with 32% protein [1], and the UVI ratio was developed with tilapia fed a similar diet [2]. Fruiting crops need more nutrients than leafy greens to support flowers and fruit, so they get a higher ratio. Rakocy et al. suggest the low end of the raft range for small plants such as Bibb lettuce and the high end for larger plants [2]. A feed with much more or less protein supplies a different amount of nitrogen per gram, so the ratio should be adjusted.

Formulas

With F = daily feed (g/day), A = grow area (m²), Rlow and Rhigh = the ends of the ratio range (g/m²/day):

Area needed: Amin = F ÷ Rhigh Amax = F ÷ Rlow Design point: Adesign = F ÷ ((Rlow + Rhigh) ÷ 2) Feed supported: Fmin = A × Rlow Fmax = A × Rhigh Actual ratio: R = F ÷ A

Fish biomass from a feeding rate p (% of body weight per day):

Biomass (kg) = F (kg/day) ÷ (p ÷ 100)

The design point uses the middle of the ratio range, the same approach as the SRAC worked example, which picks 80 g/m²/day from the 60–100 range [2]. The status line compares your actual ratio R with the range: above Rhigh means too little grow area (or too much feed), below Rlow means too much grow area (or too little feed).

Example

You feed 500 g of fish feed per day and want to grow leafy greens at 40–50 g/m²/day. The smallest area is 500 ÷ 50 = 10.0 m² and the largest is 500 ÷ 40 = 12.5 m² (about 108–135 ft²). At the middle ratio of 45 g/m²/day the design area is 500 ÷ 45 = 11.1 m². Your beds have 12 m² of growing area, so the actual ratio is 500 ÷ 12 = 41.7 g/m²/day, inside the range. Those 12 m² can use 12 × 40 = 480 to 12 × 50 = 600 g of feed per day. If the fish eat 1.5% of their body weight per day, 500 g of feed corresponds to about 0.5 ÷ 0.015 = 33.3 kg of fish.

Limitations and assumptions

  • Area, not volume. The ratio uses the planted surface area. For media beds and rafts this is the bed surface. NFT channels hold little water and are often planted more sparsely, so check their sizing against the system guide you follow.
  • Feed quality and fish species. The ranges were set for a 32% protein feed and warm-water fish such as tilapia. Higher-protein feeds release more nitrogen per gram.
  • Solids handling. Systems that remove solids early (clarifiers, swirl filters) lose some nutrients from the feed; systems with mineralization keep more. The ratio does not model this.
  • Feeding rate is a rule of thumb. The percentage of body weight eaten per day changes with fish size, species and water temperature. Use your feed supplier's chart for real feeding; the biomass result here is only an estimate.
  • Micronutrients. Even with a balanced ratio, aquaponic systems often need iron, potassium or calcium supplements [1, 2].
  • Start-up. A new system needs time for the biofilter to establish before the full feed rate is used [1].
