Before you stock shrimp

  • Check each bag's purity and hydrate form; the amounts change with both.
  • Dissolve the sodium chloride first, then each other salt separately in a bucket of system water, and the bicarbonate last. Do not mix calcium chloride with the sulfate or bicarbonate as concentrates.
  • Check salinity with a refractometer or conductivity meter, and have a lab analyse Na, K, Ca and Mg before stocking.

How the sea salt mix calculator works

Shrimp in low-salinity water need more than the right salinity: the proportions of sodium, potassium, magnesium and calcium matter too. Inland saline groundwater often has a very different make-up from diluted seawater, and is frequently short of potassium and magnesium (Boyd & Thunjai, 2003; Roy et al., 2010). Commercial sea salts fix this by copying seawater, trace elements included, but they are a large cost in indoor and inland production. The research groups at Auburn University and Kentucky State University have tested simpler mixes of six agricultural or technical-grade salts (sodium chloride, magnesium sulfate, magnesium chloride, calcium chloride, potassium chloride and sodium bicarbonate) as a cheaper replacement (Galkanda-Arachchige et al., 2020; Tierney et al., 2021; Fleckenstein et al., 2022).

This calculator builds that kind of mix for your salinity, water and salt prices:

  1. It scales the major-ion composition of natural seawater to the target salinity. In "correct my well water" mode the target salinity is the sum of the ions in your water analysis, so it adds only what is missing at the salinity you already have.
  2. Optionally it replaces the magnesium target with your own value or Mg:Ca ratio, and the alkalinity target with your own value.
  3. It subtracts what your source water already contains.
  4. From each salt's formula, IUPAC atomic weights and the purity you enter, it works out the share of each ion in the salt, then finds the amount of each ticked salt that brings every ion as close as possible to its target with a non-negative least-squares solver (Lawson & Hanson, 1974), the same method as the Nutrient Additions calculator.
  5. It multiplies by the volume, prices each salt, and compares the cost with the same salinity made from commercial sea salt.

With the six standard salts and fresh water the match is essentially exact: each salt fixes one ion, and chloride comes out within 0.2% of target. When your water already holds more of one ion than the target, or you untick a salt, the solver finds the best compromise and the tables show which ions end up over or under.

Why it matters

Salt can be one of the largest running costs of inland and indoor shrimp farming. In a 15 g/L indoor grow-out, Fleckenstein et al. (2022) found no significant differences in shrimp weight, biomass, survival, feed conversion or growth between a low-cost mixture and blends with commercial salt, and reported salt-water preparation costs of $8.83 against $12.89 per m³. Those are historical salt costs from that study, not a quote and not total production cost. Tierney et al. (2021) reported the low-cost mix as 65% cheaper by mixture weight and 57% cheaper in salt cost per kilogram of shrimp, again a salt-cost figure only. In 42-day juvenile trials at 3, 6 and 15 g/L, Galkanda-Arachchige et al. (2020) found no significant differences in survival, growth, osmoregulation or haemolymph cations between commercial salt and the low-cost mixture.

What the evidence supports is a carefully specified mix used under the study conditions. It does not establish one cheapest recipe for every water source, salinity, shrimp size and farming system, and nursery results are not proof for repeated full grow-out cycles. Getting potassium and magnesium wrong is the usual failure: survival was poorer at the lowest magnesium in controlled trials (Roy et al., 2007), and adding potassium and magnesium chlorides improved postlarval survival in West Alabama well waters (Davis et al., 2005).

Bromide, strontium, boron and fluoride (about 0.3% of sea salt) are not supplied. Seawater mass ratios follow from the table: Na:K 27.0, Mg:Ca 3.12, Mg:K 3.22, Na:Mg 8.40, Ca:K 1.03.

Share of an ion in a salt (n = ions per formula unit, A = ion mass, M = molar mass including water of crystallisation):

fraction = n × A ÷ M × purity MgCl₂·6H₂O: Mg = 24.305 ÷ 203.30 = 11.96% Cl = 2 × 35.45 ÷ 203.30 = 34.87% KCl: K = 39.098 ÷ 74.55 = 52.45%

The solver chooses salt doses x ≥ 0 (mg/L) to minimise the weighted squared relative error, with Cwater what the water already holds:

minimise Σi [ wi × (Cwater,i + Σj fractionij xj − targeti) ÷ max(targeti, 1 mg/L) ]² w = 1 for Na, K, Ca, Mg; 0.5 for Cl and alkalinity; 0.4 for SO₄

The weights are a design choice: they make the four cations fit first, because those are the ions the shrimp studies vary and measure.

salt per batch (kg) = x (mg/L) × V (L) ÷ 10⁶ salt per m³ (kg) = x ÷ 1000 cost per m³ = Σ salt per m³ × price per kg commercial salt per m³ (kg) = salinity added (g/L) × 1 total hardness (mg/L as CaCO₃) = 2.497 Ca + 4.118 Mg alkalinity: HCO₃ = alkalinity as CaCO₃ × 1.2193

The commercial comparison assumes 1 kg of commercial sea salt adds 1 kg of dissolved salinity. Real products contain some moisture and trace elements, so they may need slightly more.

Example

The defaults make 10 m³ of 5 g/L water from fresh water (no ions) with the six standard salts at 100% purity, calcium chloride as the dihydrate. Each target fixes one salt:

K 56.75 mg/L ÷ 39.098 = 1.451 mmol/L KCl × 74.55 = 108.2 mg/L Ca 58.59 mg/L ÷ 40.078 = 1.462 mmol/L CaCl₂·2H₂O × 147.01 = 214.9 mg/L SO₄ 385.66 mg/L ÷ 96.056 = 4.015 mmol/L MgSO₄·7H₂O × 246.47 = 989.5 mg/L Mg 182.53 − 97.58 (from MgSO₄) = 84.95 mg/L MgCl₂·6H₂O = 710.5 mg/L Alk 19.05 mg/L as HCO₃ = 0.312 mmol/L NaHCO₃ × 84.01 = 26.2 mg/L Na 1532.98 − 7.18 (from NaHCO₃) = 1525.8 mg/L NaCl = 3878.6 mg/L

For 10 m³ that is 38.8 kg sodium chloride, 9.90 kg magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, 7.11 kg magnesium chloride hexahydrate, 2.15 kg calcium chloride dihydrate, 1.08 kg potassium chloride and 0.26 kg sodium bicarbonate: 59.3 kg in total, of which about 9.4 kg is water of crystallisation. Chloride comes out at 2,756 mg/L, 0.1% over target, and the seven ions add up to 4.99 g/L. The calculator's solver gives the same amounts to within 0.1%. Total hardness is 898 mg/L as CaCO₃.

At the example prices (per kg: NaCl 0.20, MgSO₄·7H₂O 0.70, MgCl₂·6H₂O 0.80, CaCl₂·2H₂O 0.60, KCl 0.80, NaHCO₃ 0.60, commercial sea salt 0.90) the mix costs about 2.27 per m³ (22.68 for the batch) against 4.49 per m³ for 4.99 kg of commercial salt, about 50% less. Most of the cost is magnesium: the two magnesium salts are 29% of the mass but 56% of the cost, which is why magnesium reduction is studied. With your prices the saving may be larger or smaller.

Correcting a well water

Choose "correct my well water" and enter the analysis. The calculator takes the sum of the ions you entered as the salinity, builds the seawater profile at that salinity, and adds salts to bring the short ions up. Sodium and chloride in saline groundwater are usually already at or above the seawater proportion, so the solver mostly adds potassium chloride and magnesium salts; any ion that is already over target stays over, because salts cannot take ions out. The salinity rises slightly as the missing ions are added. Saline groundwaters differ greatly from farm to farm (Saoud et al., 2003; Boyd & Thunjai, 2003), so use a full lab analysis of your own water, not a typical value.

Limitations and assumptions

  • A starting point, not a proven recipe. The published low-cost mixes were tested under specific salinities, systems and shrimp sizes. Results differ between nursery and grow-out (Ueno Fukura et al., 2024), with the source water (Al-Subiai et al., 2025, where calcium-rich groundwater needed a higher Mg:Ca ratio), and with the system (Bajracharya et al., 2024). In a synbiotic nursery, survival was 70% with a low-cost mix against 90% in full-strength seawater, a comparison that also changed salinity (Limeira et al., 2026).
  • The published recipe is not reproduced. The exact proportions of the Auburn low-cost salt mixture are in Galkanda-Arachchige et al. (2020), the Auburn dissertation (Galkanda-Arachchige, 2021) and Alabama Extension ANR-2785, none of which could be opened when this calculator was written. Search-result summaries of the extension table list, for 1 g/L in 10,000 L, 7.7 kg sodium chloride, 1.0 kg magnesium chloride (47% MgCl₂), 1.2 kg magnesium sulfate, 213.8 g muriate of potash and 60.6 g sodium bicarbonate, with calcium chloride as a liquid. These are partial and in one repost inconsistent (213.8 kg), and the extension sheet itself says its table is accurate only for the batch of ingredients then available, so they are not offered as a preset. The calculator's seawater-ratio mix for 1 g/L agrees on sodium chloride and potassium chloride but uses more magnesium.
  • Magnesium reduction. See the caveat under the magnesium setting. Reduced magnesium lowers cost but gave lower growth in several studies, and acceptable results in others only under their conditions.
  • Major ions only. Trace elements in commercial sea salt are not supplied, and the effect of leaving them out over long periods is not well studied. Feed minerals do not replace adequate water minerals (Roy et al., 2007b; Truong et al., 2023).
  • Mass balance only. The calculation does not model ion pairs, calcium carbonate or gypsum precipitation, pH or CO₂ exchange. Salinity in g/L is treated as equal to g/kg; the difference is under 1% at 15 g/L.
  • Nitrite. Nitrite is more toxic to shrimp at lower salt and chloride levels (Sowers et al., 2004), so a lower salinity or chloride also means tighter nitrogen control.
  • Prices are editable examples. The calculator compares salt costs only, not labour, freight or the value of water reuse.
  • The ±10% band used to flag ions and the ±20% band for ratios are display rules of thumb, not shrimp tolerances.

Safety

  • Anhydrous calcium chloride releases a lot of heat as it dissolves. Add it slowly to plenty of water while stirring, and wear gloves and eye protection; the dust irritates eyes and skin.
  • Use non-iodised salt from a known supplier, and ask about any anti-caking or other additives before buying in bulk.
  • Acclimate shrimp to the new water gradually (Davis, Samocha & Boyd, 2004).
