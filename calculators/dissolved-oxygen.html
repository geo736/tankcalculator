How the dissolved oxygen calculator works

Water in contact with air takes up oxygen until it is saturated. The saturation concentration (the oxygen solubility) is the most dissolved oxygen (DO) the water holds at equilibrium with the atmosphere. It depends on three things:

  • Temperature. Warm water holds less oxygen. Fresh water at sea level holds 11.3 mg/L at 10 °C but only 7.6 mg/L at 30 °C.
  • Salinity. Dissolved salts push oxygen out. Seawater (35 ppt) holds about 17–22% less than fresh water at the same temperature (more of a drop in cold water).
  • Air pressure. Less air pressure means less oxygen pushing into the water. At high elevation the saturation value is lower in direct proportion to the pressure.

The calculator uses the equations of Benson and Krause (1980, 1984), the same equations the U.S. Geological Survey adopted in 2011 for its DOTABLES program and National Field Manual, and that Standard Methods uses in its oxygen solubility table. If you enter a measured DO reading, it also works out the percent saturation and the oxygen deficit, and compares the reading with published guidance for fish and plants.

Why dissolved oxygen matters

Low oxygen kills more pond fish than any other water quality problem, and oxygen is usually the first factor that limits how many fish a recirculating aquaculture system (RAS) or aquaponic system can carry. Fish, the nitrifying bacteria in the biofilter and plant roots all use oxygen at the same time. Knowing the saturation value tells you two things: the best you can do with aeration alone (you cannot get above 100% with air), and how far below saturation your water is (the deficit), which drives how fast an aerator can add oxygen.

Warm water is a double problem: it holds less oxygen while fish and bacteria breathe faster. That is why summer nights, when algae stop producing oxygen, are the riskiest time in ponds (Boyd & Tucker, 1998).

Formulas

1. Fresh water at 1 atm (Benson & Krause, 1984; USGS, 2011). T is temperature in kelvin (T = t + 273.15):

ln C₀ = −139.34411 + 1.575701×10⁵/T − 6.642308×10⁷/T² + 1.243800×10¹⁰/T³ − 8.621949×10¹¹/T⁴ (C₀ in mg/L)

2. Salinity correction, S in parts per thousand (practical salinity):

Fs = exp[ −S × (0.017674 − 10.754/T + 2140.7/T²) ]

3. Pressure correction, P = barometric pressure in atmospheres, t in °C:

ln Pwv = 11.8571 − 3840.70/T − 216961/T² (water vapor pressure, atm) θ = 0.000975 − 1.426×10⁻⁵ t + 6.436×10⁻⁸ t² Fp = P × (1 − Pwv/P)(1 − θP) / [ (1 − Pwv)(1 − θ) ]

4. Saturation, percent saturation and deficit:

Csat = C₀ × Fs × Fp % sat = 100 × DOmeasured / Csat Deficit = Csat − DOmeasured

Pressure from elevation (U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976), h in meters:

P = 101.325 kPa × (1 − 2.25577×10⁻⁵ × h)^5.25588

This gives the average pressure at that elevation. Weather moves real pressure up and down by a few percent, so a barometer reading at the site is more accurate. 1 atm = 760 mm Hg = 101.325 kPa = 1013.25 hPa.

Example

Fresh water (S = 0) at 25 °C at sea level (1 atm). T = 298.15 K, so ln C₀ = 2.1119 and C₀ = 8.26 mg/L. Fs = 1 and Fp = 1, so the saturation concentration is 8.26 mg/L, matching the USGS and Standard Methods tables (8.26 mg/L at 25 °C, 9.09 mg/L at 20 °C).

A meter reads 6.5 mg/L. Percent saturation = 6.5 ÷ 8.26 × 100 = 78.7%, and the oxygen deficit is 8.26 − 6.5 = 1.76 mg/L. For warm-water fish this is above the 5 mg/L level usually given as desirable, so the status is OK.

The same water at 20 °C and 1,000 m elevation: the standard atmosphere gives 89.9 kPa (674 mm Hg, 0.887 atm), and saturation drops from 9.09 to about 8.04 mg/L.

Guidance for measured DO

These are guidelines, not hard limits. Tolerance varies by species, size, acclimation and how long the low oxygen lasts.

GroupLow (danger)MarginalOK
Warm-water fish (tilapia, catfish, carp)< 3 mg/L3–5 mg/L≥ 5 mg/L
Cold-water fish (trout, salmon)< 4 mg/L4–6.5 mg/L≥ 6.5 mg/L
Hydroponic plants (root zone)< 3 mg/L–≥ 3 mg/L

Where the numbers come from: Boyd's widely used table for warm-water pond fish describes 1.5–5 mg/L as survivable but stressful, with slow growth, and above 5 mg/L as desirable (Boyd & Tucker, 1998). The U.S. EPA (1986) freshwater criteria give a 1-day minimum of 3.0 mg/L for warm-water and 4.0 mg/L for cold-water fish (other than early life stages) and a 30-day mean of 5.5 and 6.5 mg/L. The FAO aquaponics manual (Somerville et al., 2014, Table 3.1) lists 4–6 mg/L for warm-water fish, 6–8 mg/L for cold-water fish, more than 3 mg/L for plants and 4–8 mg/L for nitrifying bacteria. The cut-offs above combine these sources and should be treated as rules of thumb. Eggs and fry need more oxygen than the values shown, and in aquaponics it is common to aim for at least 5 mg/L so that the biofilter bacteria are not limited either.

Limitations and assumptions

  • Equilibrium with air. The result is the saturation value for water in contact with normal air. Pure-oxygen injection, strong algae photosynthesis or air entrained under pressure can push DO above 100%. Strong total gas supersaturation can cause gas bubble disease in fish.
  • Validity range. The equations were fitted for 0–40 °C and salinity 0–40. The calculator does not compute outside these ranges.
  • Salinity units. Salinity is entered as practical salinity (PSU), which is numerically almost the same as parts per thousand (g/kg) for natural waters.
  • Water vapor. The vapor pressure term uses the fresh-water equation for every salinity, as in the USGS method. Salt lowers the vapor pressure slightly; the effect on the result is very small.
  • Units. mg/L is used throughout. In fresh water, 1 mg/L ≈ 1 ppm.
  • Meter accuracy. Percent saturation is only as good as the DO reading. Calibrate the meter as its manual describes, and measure at dawn to see the daily low.
