How the pipe water velocity calculator works

Water velocity in a full pipe is simply the flow rate divided by the open area of the bore. The same flow moves much faster through a small pipe than a large one, because the area grows with the square of the diameter: halving the inside diameter makes the water four times faster.

Pick a standard PVC pipe from the list (its real inside diameter is filled in for you) or enter any inside diameter. The calculator gives the velocity, the Reynolds number and flow regime, checks the velocity against the minimum and maximum you set, and lists the smallest Schedule 40 and Schedule 80 pipe that stays at or below your maximum. In Pipe size needed mode it works backwards from a target velocity to the inside diameter you need and the smallest standard pipe that meets it.

Why velocity matters in fish and plant systems

  • Too fast in pump discharge lines wastes energy (friction loss rises roughly with the square of velocity), makes noise, and raises the pressure surge (water hammer) when a valve closes or a pump stops. Surge is the main reason PVC pipe guidance caps velocity.
  • Too slow in lines that carry fish waste, uneaten feed or root debris lets solids settle and build up, which can clog drains and create low-oxygen pockets.
  • Pipe size is a cost trade-off. Bigger pipe is slower and cheaper to pump through but costs more to buy. A velocity check is the quickest way to land in the middle.

Formulas

For a full pipe of inside diameter D carrying flow Q:

Area A = π × D² ÷ 4 Velocity v = Q ÷ A Diameter for a target velocity: D = √(4 Q ÷ (π v))

The Reynolds number compares inertial and viscous forces. ν is the kinematic viscosity of water (dynamic viscosity μ divided by density ρ):

Re = v × D ÷ ν ν = μ ÷ ρ

For flow in circular pipes, the usual engineering limits are: laminar below Re ≈ 2,300, transitional from about 2,300 to 4,000, and turbulent above about 4,000 (Çengel & Cimbala). Almost every pump or drain line in a real system is turbulent.

PVC pipe inside diameters

ASTM D1785 fixes the outside diameter and the minimum wall thickness of Schedule 40 and 80 PVC pipe; the outside diameter is the same for both schedules, so Schedule 80 has a smaller bore. The calculator uses inside diameter = outside diameter − 2 × minimum wall.

Recommended velocity ranges (rules of thumb)

There is no single correct velocity. These are commonly used design figures, not hard limits, and the calculator lets you change both:

  • Maximum about 5 ft/s (1.5 m/s) for PVC pressure lines, and never more than 10 ft/s (3 m/s). University of Florida IFAS extension guidance gives these limits to keep water hammer surges within the pressure rating of plastic pipe (Clark & Haman). Short runs on the suction side of a pump are usually kept slower still.
  • Minimum about 2 ft/s (0.6 m/s) for lines that carry solids. This is the self-cleansing velocity that sewer design standards require for pipes flowing full (Ten States Standards). Fish-tank drains and solids lines carry lighter material than sewage, so some designers accept less, but going much below 1 ft/s (0.3 m/s) invites settling. Clean-water lines (for example after a filter) have no practical minimum.

The gauge in the visualization shows these zones: blue below your minimum, green between minimum and maximum, amber above your maximum.

Example

A pump moves 20 US gpm through 1¼″ Schedule 40 PVC at 20 °C.

Q = 20 gpm × 3.785411784 L ÷ 60 s = 1.2618 L/s = 0.0012618 m³/s D = 1.380 in = 0.035052 m A = π × 0.035052² ÷ 4 = 0.00096497 m² (1.496 in², 9.65 cm²) v = 0.0012618 ÷ 0.00096497 = 1.308 m/s = 4.29 ft/s ν = 1.002 × 10⁻³ ÷ 998.21 = 1.004 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s Re = 1.308 × 0.035052 ÷ 1.004 × 10⁻⁶ ≈ 45,660 → turbulent

4.29 ft/s is between the 2 ft/s minimum and the 5 ft/s maximum, so this pipe is a good fit. The 1″ Schedule 40 pipe (ID 1.049 in) would run at 7.42 ft/s, over the limit, so 1¼″ is the smallest Schedule 40 size that works. In Schedule 80, 1¼″ (ID 1.278 in) gives 5.002 ft/s, just over 5 ft/s, so the smallest Schedule 80 size is 1½″ (3.63 ft/s). At 5 ft/s, 1¼″ Schedule 40 can carry up to about 23.3 gpm.

Things the calculator does not include

  • Partly full pipes. The formulas assume the pipe runs full. A gravity drain that runs half full has half the flow area in use, so its real velocity at the same flow is higher and depends on slope; use Manning's equation for open-channel flow.
  • Friction loss and pump head. Velocity is the first step; head loss also depends on pipe length, fittings and roughness.
  • Other pipe types. Thin-wall, SDR-rated, polyethylene and metric pipes have different inside diameters. Use the custom inside diameter for these.
  • Water hammer itself. The 5 ft/s guideline reduces surge; it does not replace a surge check for long lines or fast-closing valves.
