Visualization

How the hydroponic air pump and air stone calculator works

Plant roots respire: they take in oxygen dissolved in the nutrient solution. Where roots sit in solution all the time, an air pump pushes air to air stones (diffusers) on the floor, and the rising bubbles dissolve oxygen and stir the solution. The calculator sizes that air in five steps:

  1. Which solution is aerated. The system type decides. In DWC (deep water culture, raft canals) and SWC the roots hang in the canals or troughs, so those are aerated. In NFT, media beds and other recirculating systems only the reservoir or sump is aerated.
  2. Air flow. Aeration intensity (litres of air per litre of solution per minute) × solution volume × safety factor.
  3. Number of stones. Two counts: enough stones to pass the air at their rated flow, and enough to keep within the largest spacing you set. The larger count is used, and the page says which one decided it.
  4. Pressure. The pump must push air down to the stones against the solution above them, plus the pressure drop across the stone and the losses in the air line.
  5. Pump and power. The preset pump (or yours) that gives enough air at that pressure, from its curve, and the electricity it uses.

The physics, stone presets and pump curves are shared with the fish-system air pump calculator, so the two pages give the same pressure and pump results for the same stones and depth.

Why root-zone oxygen matters

Roots that run short of oxygen take up less water and nutrients, grow slowly and are more open to root rot. The FAO aquaponics manual lists more than 3 mg/L of dissolved oxygen as the requirement for plants (Somerville et al., 2014, Table 3.1), and the Dissolved Oxygen calculator uses that figure. Warm solution makes it harder in two ways: it holds less oxygen (8.3 mg/L at 25 °C against 9.1 mg/L at 20 °C at sea level), and roots respire faster. In a deep-water lettuce trial, Carotti et al. (2021) found about 28 °C best for growth but called 24 °C for both air and roots a reasonable compromise that avoids heating the solution. The page warns above 26 °C, the same flag as the Design a hydroponic system guide.

How each system gets oxygen to the roots:

  • DWC (deep water culture). Rafts float on canals usually 20–40 cm deep and the roots are submerged all the time, so the air stones in the canals are the main oxygen supply. FAO TP 589 describes DWC canals aerated with air stones (Somerville et al., 2014). If the air pump stops, oxygen in the canals falls; plan backup power with the Battery Life calculator.
  • SWC (shallow water culture). On this page, SWC means shallow troughs or rafts with a few cm up to about 15 cm of solution (a rule of thumb used here to separate it from DWC). The maths is the same as DWC, but the stones are very shallow, so the pump needs little pressure and each litre of air dissolves less oxygen.
  • NFT (nutrient film technique). A thin film of solution, a few millimetres deep, runs along the floor of each channel. The roots form a mat: the bottom of the mat sits in the moving film, the top is in the moist air of the channel, so roots take oxygen from that air gap (Cooper, 1979; Maucieri et al., 2019). The channels themselves are not aerated. An air stone in the reservoir keeps the solution that returns to the channels close to saturation and keeps the reservoir mixed.
  • Media bed / flood-and-drain. Each time the bed drains, air is drawn down into the media around the roots (Somerville et al., 2014). The beds are not aerated; a stone in the sump keeps the stored solution oxygenated between floods.
  • Reservoir only. Drip and Dutch-bucket systems, a separate DWC sump or a mixing tank: only the tank is aerated.

Why aeration intensity, not oxygen demand

The fish-system calculator works from oxygen demand, because fish oxygen use per kg of fish or feed is well published. For plants there is no comparable planning figure. Root respiration has been measured, for example in lettuce at 23 °C by Chun & Takakura (1994), but per millilitre of root volume, and root volume per plant changes from day to day as the crop grows and is rarely known. Turning that into air flow would also need the oxygen transfer of the stones in nutrient solution, which is not published for small air stones. So this page sizes air from aeration intensity: Baiyin et al. (2021) grew plants hydroponically at different intensities and found that growth rose between 0.07 and 0.15 L of air per L of solution per minute, changed little from 0.15 to 1.18, and fell from 1.18 to 2.35. More air is not always better, and it costs electricity. Their tests were in small containers, so treat the result as a starting point and check with a DO meter.

As a check, the page also estimates how much oxygen the chosen air flow can dissolve (in clean water, see Formulas). It is an upper estimate, not a sizing figure.

Formulas

1. Aerated solution volume (L):

Canals / troughs: V = length × width × solution depth × number (m³ × 1000) Reservoir / sump: V = reservoir volume; floor area = V ÷ solution depth

2. Air flow (L/min):

Design intensity i = intensity × safety factor (L air per L per min) Air (L/min) = i × V Per canal = Air ÷ number of canals 1 CFM = 28.317 L/min; 1 m³/h = 16.667 L/min

3. Stones (s = largest spacing):

By flow: Nflow = round up (air per canal ÷ flow per stone) By spacing: canal: Nspace = round up (L ÷ s) × round up (W ÷ s) reservoir: Nspace = round up (floor area ÷ s²) Stones = the larger of Nflow and Nspace (porous tube: metres = round up (air ÷ flow per m), or runs s apart × length)

4. Pressure at the pump (gauge; ρ = 998 kg/m³, g = 9.80665 m/s²):

P = ρ × g × stone depth + stone drop + line allowance 1 kPa = 0.145 psi = 4.01 in H₂O = 10.2 cm H₂O

5. Pump and energy. Each preset pump’s curve is a straight line from its free-air flow through its rated point, cut off at its maximum pressure. Pumps needed = round up (air ÷ flow per pump at P). The suggestion is the preset with the lowest total power that needs no more than 4 pumps (if none can, the lowest total power overall). Energy = watts × 24 h per day, × 365 per year.

6. Saturation and oxygen check. Csat is the DO saturation of fresh water at the solution temperature and the site’s air pressure (Benson & Krause, 1984; USGS, 2011; U.S. Standard Atmosphere for pressure), as in the Dissolved Oxygen calculator. The oxygen the air can dissolve uses the same standard-to-field conversion as the fish-system page, with α = 1 (clean water):

OTE = SOTE per m × stone depth × 1.024(T − 20) × (Csat − C) ÷ 9.09 O₂ (g/h) = Air (L/min) × 60 × 0.2787 g O₂ per L of air × OTE

Example (the default inputs, DWC)

Two raft canals, each 2.4 m × 1.2 m with 30 cm of solution, at 20 °C and sea level, minimum DO 3 mg/L. Intensity 0.07 L/L/min, safety factor 1.5, fine-bubble 10 cm stones (10 L/min, 2 kPa drop, 5%/m) on the floor, largest spacing 1.2 m, 1 kPa line allowance.

  • Volume: 2.4 × 1.2 × 0.30 = 0.864 m³ = 864 L per canal, 1,728 L in all.
  • Design intensity 0.07 × 1.5 = 0.105 L/L/min, inside the 0.07–0.15 range. Air = 0.105 × 1,728 = 181.4 L/min (6.41 CFM, 10.89 m³/h), or 90.7 L/min per canal.
  • Stones by flow: 90.7 ÷ 10 = 9.07 → 10 per canal. By spacing: 2.4 ÷ 1.2 = 2 along × 1.2 ÷ 1.2 = 1 across = 2. Flow governs: 10 stones per canal, 20 in all, each at 9.07 L/min.
  • Pressure: 998 × 9.80665 × 0.30 = 2.94 kPa of water + 2 kPa stone + 1 kPa line = 5.94 kPa (0.86 psi, 23.8 in H₂O, 60.5 cm H₂O).
  • At 5.94 kPa a 60 L/min linear pump gives about 83.3 L/min, so 3 pumps, 150 W (3.6 kWh/day, 1,314 kWh/year). Two 120 L/min pumps would need 220 W; aquarium pumps would need 40 (160 W).
  • Saturation at 20 °C: 9.09 mg/L; 3 mg/L is 33% of it. Oxygen check: SOTE 5% × 0.3 m = 1.5%; × (9.09 − 3) ÷ 9.09 = 1.0%; 181.4 × 60 × 0.2787 × 0.0100 ≈ 30.5 g/h (≈ 732 g/day, 17.6 mg/L per hour of solution) in clean water.

The other system types start from these defaults when you pick them (same intensity, safety factor, stones and line allowance):

  • SWC: two 2.4 × 1.2 m troughs with 10 cm of solution: 576 L → 60.5 L/min (2.14 CFM); 30.2 L/min per trough → 4 stones per trough by flow (2 by spacing); 0.98 + 2 + 1 = 3.98 kPa. Suggested: one 60 L/min linear pump (50 W, about 88 L/min at this pressure); 12 aquarium pumps would save only 2 W.
  • NFT: a 200 L reservoir with 50 cm of solution: 21 L/min (0.74 CFM); 3 stones by flow (1 by spacing for 0.4 m² of floor); 4.89 + 2 + 1 = 7.89 kPa; suggested: one 60 L/min linear pump (50 W); 6 aquarium pumps (24 W) would use less power but are more to run.
  • Media bed: a 300 L sump with 50 cm of solution: 31.5 L/min; 4 stones; 7.89 kPa; suggested: one 60 L/min linear pump (50 W), or 8 aquarium pumps (32 W).
  • Reservoir only: 100 L with 40 cm of solution: 10.5 L/min; 2 stones; 3.91 + 2 + 1 = 6.91 kPa; 3 aquarium pumps (12 W).

Stone spacing (rule of thumb)

There is no published standard for how far apart air stones in a raft canal should be. The default of 1.2 m comes from one well-documented design: in the University of the Virgin Islands (UVI) commercial aquaponic system, each raft tank had 24 air diffusers of 10 L/min, placed every 1.2 m along the centre of the tank (Rakocy et al., 2004). Wider canals get more than one row on this page (one row per 1.2 m of width), which is this page’s own extension. For a reservoir, the page allows one stone per 1.2 m × 1.2 m of floor. Small stones in big canals leave dead corners; if spacing governs, consider porous tubing along the canal instead of many stones.

Typical stone values and pump presets

The stone and pump presets are the same as on the fish-system calculator, which explains where each comes from. They are typical, illustrative values: replace them with your stone’s and pump’s data. Small air pumps lose flow quickly as pressure rises, so always read the pump’s curve at the pressure shown here, not its free-air rating. The suggestion avoids more than 4 pumps where it can; the “Pump options” line shows every alternative and its power.

Limitations and assumptions

  • Intensity is a growth trial result, not a design standard. Baiyin et al. (2021) worked with small containers; long canals with many plants, warm solution or dense roots may need more. Measure DO in the root zone, especially at the far end of long canals and in warm weather.
  • NFT and media beds. The intensity is applied to the reservoir or sump only. It keeps the stored solution oxygenated; the roots themselves get most of their oxygen from air in the channel or media.
  • Oxygen estimate. It assumes clean water (α = 1) and the stone preset’s SOTE per metre, which is a rule of thumb; real nutrient solution and very shallow stones will transfer less. It does not include oxygen entering at the surface or used by the roots.
  • Spacing is a rule of thumb from one design; layout, canal shape and the flow of solution along the canal all change mixing.
  • Pressure. Long, narrow or many-branched air lines can lose more than the allowance; gang valves and check valves add drop.
  • Air conditions. Saturation uses the site’s average pressure. Pump ratings are usually at sea level.
  • Fish. In aquaponics the fish tanks need their own air, sized from oxygen demand with the fish-system calculator.
