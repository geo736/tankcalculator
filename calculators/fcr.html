How the FCR calculator works

The feed conversion ratio (FCR) is the weight of feed given divided by the weight the fish gained. An FCR of 1.5 means 1.5 kg of feed was used for every 1 kg of fish produced, so lower is better. Robinson & Li (2015) define it the same way for catfish: pounds of feed fed per pound of weight gain.

In Measure mode you enter what happened over one period: the fish at the start, the fish at the end, any fish removed alive and any that died, the feed used and the number of days. Each group of fish can be entered as a number of fish times their average weight, or as a total weight. The calculator then works out two FCRs and a set of related growth and efficiency figures.

In Plan mode you enter how much fish weight you want to grow and an FCR, and it gives the feed you will need. Add a feed price and a second, better FCR to see what improving feed conversion is worth in feed, money and ammonia.

Biological FCR and economic FCR

Fish that die during the period still ate feed and still grew before they died. The two FCRs treat them differently:

  • Biological FCR counts all the growth the fish made, including the weight of fish that died. It tells you how well the fish turned feed into flesh.
  • Economic FCR counts only the weight you can sell or still have in the tank. Dead fish are lost, so the feed they ate is charged to the surviving fish. It tells you what the feed really cost you per kilogram of product.

Jouannais et al. (2023) use these definitions in their study of trout farming: biological FCR is feed divided by the production of live plus dead fish, and economic FCR is feed divided by live fish only. Economic FCR is always the same or higher than biological FCR, and the gap between them grows with mortality. Not every author uses the terms the same way, so when you compare your FCR with a published figure, check which one it is. Fry et al. (2018) also point out that FCR compares weight of feed with weight of fish, not nutrients, so it is a poor way to compare very different species.

Why FCR matters

Feed is the largest running cost. In semi-intensive and intensive aquaculture, feed typically accounts for 40–60% of production costs (Shipton & Hasan, 2013). Feed cost per kilogram of fish is simply feed price × FCR, so a 10% better FCR is a 10% lower feed bill for the same harvest. Robinson & Li (2015) show the same effect for pond catfish with a drop in farm-level FCR from 2.5 to 2.0. To compare an investment that improves FCR (better feed, oxygen, automatic feeders) against its cost, use the Cost Benefit Analysis calculator.

FCR sets the waste load. Every kilogram of feed that does not become fish ends up as ammonia, carbon dioxide and solids in the water. A common design estimate for the total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) the fish excrete is feed × protein × 0.092 (Timmons et al., 2002; Ebeling, Timmons & Bisogni, 2006). For the same harvest, a higher FCR means more feed, so a bigger biofilter and more oxygen. Size the biofilter with the Biofilter Surface Area calculator and the daily ration with the Feeding Rate calculator. In aquaponics, wasted feed is not lost to the plants, but uneaten feed that rots in the tank costs oxygen and water quality.

What makes FCR worse

  • Overfeeding and uneaten feed. Pellets that sink uneaten count as feed fed but produce no fish. Feed what the fish clear in a few minutes.
  • Low dissolved oxygen. Fish eat less and digest less efficiently when oxygen is low, and stressed fish waste more feed.
  • Temperature away from the optimum. Each species has a temperature range where it grows best. In cooler water fish eat less, and feeding the same ration leaves more uneaten.
  • Poor or stale feed. Low-quality ingredients, the wrong pellet size, or feed stored too long, hot or damp (losing vitamins and fat quality) all raise FCR.
  • Mortality, especially of large fish late in the cycle. Their feed is lost, which raises economic FCR. Jouannais et al. (2023) found that the same mortality rate costs much more in late growth stages than early ones.
  • Large fish. FCR rises as fish grow, so the last weeks before harvest are the least efficient (Robinson & Li, 2015).
  • Inaccurate records. A wrong average weight, uncounted dead fish or a missed feed bag can move FCR more than any real change. Weigh a large enough sample of fish and record feed by the bag.

Formulas

All weights in the same unit (the calculator works in kilograms). B0 = biomass at the start, B1 = biomass at the end, R = weight of fish removed alive during the period, D = weight of dead fish, F = feed fed, t = days.

Biomass (if entered as fish) = number of fish × average weight Biological gain Gbio = B1 + R + D − B0 Economic gain Geco = B1 + R − B0 Biological FCR = F ÷ Gbio Economic FCR = F ÷ Geco

Feed efficiency is the inverse of FCR, written as a percentage:

Feed efficiency (%) = 100 × Gbio ÷ F = 100 ÷ biological FCR

Protein efficiency ratio (PER) is the weight gained per unit of protein fed, a measure first proposed by Osborne, Mendel & Ferry (1919):

Protein fed = F × protein % ÷ 100 PER = Gbio ÷ protein fed

Specific growth rate (SGR) uses the average weight of one fish at the start (W0) and the end (W1). Aquaculture papers usually report it as 100 times the instantaneous growth rate g (Hopkins, 1992). Crane, Ogle & Shoup (2020) point out that the true percentage weight gain per day is 100 × (eg − 1), which is slightly higher; the calculator shows both.

g = (ln W1 − ln W0) ÷ t SGR (usual form) = 100 × g %/day Daily compound growth = 100 × (eg − 1) %/day

Average daily feed as a percentage of body weight uses the mean of the starting and ending biomass, a simple approximation:

Daily feed = F ÷ t Feed % BW / day = 100 × (F ÷ t) ÷ ((B0 + B1) ÷ 2)

Mortality uses fish numbers: dead fish ÷ fish at the start × 100. If every count is entered, the calculator also checks that start − removed − dead = end and warns about fish that are unaccounted for.

Plan mode and costs:

Feed needed = biomass gain × FCR Feed cost per kg fish = feed price per kg × FCR Feed saved = biomass gain × (FCRcurrent − FCRimproved) TAN from feed = feed × protein % ÷ 100 × 0.092

Typical FCR values

FCR depends on species, fish size, feed, temperature and management, so the values below are reference points, not targets. Only figures we could trace to a named source are listed; for other species, including rainbow trout, use your feed supplier's figures or your own records.

Example (the calculator's default inputs)

A tilapia tank starts the period with 1,000 fish averaging 50 g, so B0 = 50 kg. Over 120 days the fish eat 300 kg of 32% protein feed. During the period 60 fish averaging 200 g are harvested early (R = 12 kg) and 40 fish averaging 120 g die (D = 4.8 kg). At the end 900 fish averaging 250 g remain, so B1 = 225 kg. The counts reconcile: 1,000 − 60 − 40 = 900.

  • Biological gain = 225 + 12 + 4.8 − 50 = 191.8 kg; biological FCR = 300 ÷ 191.8 = 1.56.
  • Economic gain = 225 + 12 − 50 = 187 kg; economic FCR = 300 ÷ 187 = 1.60, inside the 1.4–2.0 tilapia range of Lutz (2000).
  • Feed efficiency = 100 × 191.8 ÷ 300 = 63.9%.
  • Protein fed = 300 × 0.32 = 96 kg; PER = 191.8 ÷ 96 = 2.00.
  • g = ln(250 ÷ 50) ÷ 120 = 0.01341; SGR = 1.34%/day (daily compound growth 1.35%/day).
  • Daily feed = 300 ÷ 120 = 2.5 kg; mean biomass = (50 + 225) ÷ 2 = 137.5 kg; feed = 1.82% of body weight per day.
  • Mortality = 40 ÷ 1,000 = 4%.
  • With feed at an example price of 1.20 per kg, feed cost = 1.20 × 1.604 = 1.93 per kg of fish.
  • TAN = 300 × 0.32 × 0.092 = 8.83 kg of ammonia-N over the period, about 47 g per kg of fish harvested.

In Plan mode the defaults are 1,000 kg of fish to grow, an FCR of 1.6 now and 1.3 after improvement, 32% protein feed at 1.20 per kg. Feed needed is 1,000 × 1.6 = 1,600 kg now and 1,300 kg after improvement, so improving FCR saves 300 kg of feed, or 360 in money (1.92 → 1.56 per kg of fish). Each 0.1 of FCR is 100 kg of feed (120 in money). TAN falls from 47.1 kg to 38.27 kg.

Limitations and assumptions

  • No fish added during the period. If you stocked fish part way through, add their weight to the starting fish, or the FCR will look better than it is.
  • Feed fed, not feed eaten. FCR uses the feed you put in. Uneaten feed is part of the result, which is what makes FCR useful for spotting overfeeding.
  • Dead fish weight. If dead fish were not weighed, the biological gain is an estimate. Fish lost without being found (predators, decomposed fish) cannot be included at all; the calculator warns you when counts do not add up.
  • As-fed basis. Feed weight is as fed, including its moisture. Some studies, including the Norwegian salmon reports, also give dry-matter FCR, which is lower.
  • Growth rate and feed % BW are averages. SGR assumes exponential growth between two average weights, and the % BW figure uses the mean of starting and ending biomass. Both get less accurate over long periods, so measure every few weeks.
  • Reference values are context, not standards. They come from particular systems and years.
