How the air pump and air stone calculator works

Fish, and the bacteria in the biofilter, use oxygen all the time. An air pump pushes air down to air stones (diffusers); the bubbles rise and a small part of their oxygen dissolves into the water on the way up. The calculator works backwards from the oxygen the tanks need to the air that must be pumped:

  1. Oxygen demand. Either the daily feed × the oxygen used per kg of feed (this includes the fish, nitrifying bacteria and the bacteria breaking down waste), or the fish weight × an oxygen use rate per kg of fish (fish only). It is multiplied by your safety factor, and by the share you want air to supply.
  2. Standard oxygen transfer. Diffusers are rated by their standard oxygen transfer efficiency (SOTE): the share of the oxygen in the air that dissolves in clean water at 20 °C with no oxygen in it yet (the test conditions of ASCE/EWRI 2-06). For bubble diffusers it grows roughly in proportion to depth, so it is entered per metre of submergence.
  3. Field transfer. Your tank is not clean water at 20 °C and 0 mg/L. The standard figure is corrected for the culture water (α), temperature (θT−20), and above all for the dissolved oxygen you want to keep: the closer to saturation, the slower oxygen moves in.
  4. Air flow. Oxygen needed ÷ (oxygen carried by each litre of air × field transfer efficiency).
  5. Stones and pump. The air flow per tank ÷ the flow each stone is designed for gives the number of stones. The pump must push air down to the stones against the water above them, plus the pressure drop across the stone and the losses in the air line. The calculator then picks the preset pump (or checks yours) that gives enough air at that pressure, and works out its power use.

Why it matters

Dissolved oxygen (DO) is usually the first thing that limits how many fish a tank can hold, and low DO kills fish faster than any other water quality problem (Boyd & Tucker, 1998). Air pumps are cheap and simple, but they have two weak points. First, they only get a small share of the oxygen into the water, so the air flow needed climbs quickly with fish weight. Second, when the power fails, oxygen in a heavily stocked tank can fall to dangerous levels in a short time. SRAC Publication 452 says backup power is essential in recirculating systems (Masser et al., 1999). Use the Battery Life calculator with the pump power found here to size a backup battery.

The pressure the pump must make is just as important as its flow. Linear (diaphragm) air pumps deliver much less air as the pressure rises, and stop at their maximum pressure. A pump rated “60 L/min” may give far less at the bottom of a deep tank. Deeper stones dissolve more oxygen but need more pressure; see the Hydrostatic Pressure calculator.

Formulas

1. Oxygen demand (g O₂/h):

From feed: D = feed (kg/day) × F (kg O₂/kg feed) × 1000 ÷ 24 From biomass: D = biomass (kg) × R (mg O₂/kg/h) ÷ 1000 Design: Dair = D × safety factor × share supplied by air

2. Field oxygen transfer efficiency (standard → field). Csat is the DO saturation at your temperature, salinity and site pressure (Benson & Krause, 1984, as in the Dissolved Oxygen calculator), C is the DO you want to keep, and Cs20 = 9.09 mg/L is saturation in fresh water at 20 °C and 1 atm:

SOTE = SOTE per m × depth (m) (capped at 40%) OTEf = SOTE × α × 1.024(T − 20) × (Csat − C) ÷ Cs20

This is the standard way of converting aerator ratings to pond or tank conditions (ASCE, 2007; Boyd, 1998). The salinity (β) and pressure corrections are already inside Csat, because it is calculated at the real salinity and elevation.

3. Oxygen in the air. Dry air is 20.95% oxygen by volume (U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976), which is 23.1% by mass (molar masses 31.999 g/mol for O₂ and 28.964 g/mol for air). The density of air comes from the ideal gas law at the site’s pressure and 20 °C:

ρair = P × 0.0289644 ÷ (8.314462618 × 293.15) (kg/m³ = g/L; 1.204 at sea level) O₂ per litre of air = ρair × 0.2314 (0.279 g/L at sea level)

4. Air flow and diffusers:

Air (L/min) = Dair ÷ (O₂ per L of air × OTEf) ÷ 60 Per tank = Air ÷ number of tanks Stones/tank = round up (per tank ÷ flow per stone)

5. Pressure at the pump (gauge pressure; ρw = 998 kg/m³ fresh to 1025 kg/m³ at 35 ppt, g = 9.80665 m/s²):

P = ρw × g × depth + diffuser drop + line allowance 1 kPa = 0.145 psi = 4.01 in H₂O = 10.2 cm H₂O

6. Pump. Each preset pump’s curve is a straight line from its free-air flow (0 pressure) through its rated point, cut off at its maximum pressure. Pumps needed = round up (air flow ÷ flow per pump at P). Energy per day = watts × 24 h.

Example (the default inputs)

Two 1,000 L tanks hold 50 kg of fish fed 1 kg of feed a day. Water is fresh, 25 °C, at sea level; we want to keep 5 mg/L. Fine-bubble 10 cm stones sit 0.9 m deep; SOTE 5% per m, α = 0.8, safety factor 1.5, air is the only oxygen source.

  • Demand: 1 kg × 0.5 = 0.5 kg O₂/day = 20.83 g/h; × 1.5 = 31.25 g/h (0.75 kg/day).
  • Csat at 25 °C = 8.26 mg/L; Cs20 = 9.09 mg/L; 1.024⁵ = 1.126. Field factor = 0.8 × 1.126 × (8.26 − 5) ÷ 9.09 = 0.323.
  • SOTE = 5% × 0.9 = 4.5%, so OTEf = 4.5% × 0.323 = 1.45%. Each m³ of air (0.279 kg O₂) gives 4.05 g of dissolved oxygen.
  • Air = 31.25 ÷ (0.2787 g/L × 0.01455) ÷ 60 = 128.5 L/min (4.54 CFM, 7.71 m³/h), or 64.2 L/min per tank: 7 stones per tank at 10 L/min (14 total).
  • Pressure: 998 × 9.80665 × 0.9 = 8.81 kPa of water + 2 kPa stone + 1 kPa line = 11.81 kPa (1.71 psi, 47.4 in H₂O, 120 cm H₂O).
  • At 11.81 kPa a 60 L/min linear pump gives about 67.6 L/min, so 2 pumps (100 W, 2.4 kWh/day). One 120 L/min pump would give 129.0 L/min at 110 W, just enough but with nothing in hand and using more power.
  • Checks: 25 kg/m³ is within the ≈ 30 kg/m³ air-only rule of thumb; 64 L/min of air per m³ of water.

Air per water volume

There is no published standard for how much air a fish tank should get per volume of water. As a reference only, Texas rules for wastewater aeration basins require at least 20 standard ft³/min of air per 1,000 ft³ for coarse-bubble mixing (30 TAC §217.155), which is 20 L/min per m³. A fish tank does not need to keep sludge suspended, so this is not a requirement here; it just gives a sense of scale. Above about 100 L/min per m³ (this page’s own flag, a rule of thumb) the tank is very turbulent and air alone is becoming impractical.

When air is not enough

Because air is only about 21% oxygen and a shallow stone dissolves only a few percent of it, the air flow needed rises steeply with stocking density. A common rule of thumb in RAS engineering is that aeration with air can support about 30 kg of fish per m³; above that, pure oxygen is usually needed (Ebeling, 2000; see Fish Stocking Density). The calculator warns above this density. To size air for only part of the demand (for example, when an oxygen cone covers the rest), lower the share supplied by air.

Limitations and assumptions

  • Maker curves rule. Pump flow at pressure, stone flow and pressure drop all come from the manufacturer. The presets are examples.
  • SOTE varies. It changes with bubble size, air flow per stone (more air per stone makes bigger bubbles and lower efficiency), stone fouling, tank shape and water quality. In tests of diffused-air systems for aquaculture, Boyd & Moore (1993) found that efficiency fell as the air flow rose and that many diffusers are needed to use the air well. The per-metre rule is approximate, works best between about 0.5 and 5 m, and is capped at 40%.
  • Alpha depends on oils, surfactants, dissolved organic matter and feed; there is no single value for fish tanks.
  • Saturation at depth is a little higher than at the surface because of the water pressure on the bubbles. The calculator ignores this, which is slightly conservative for shallow tanks.
  • Steady state. Demand peaks after feeding and in warm weather. The safety factor covers these, and you should measure DO.
  • Air conditions. Air density uses the site’s average pressure (U.S. Standard Atmosphere) and 20 °C air. Pump ratings are usually at sea level; pumps deliver less mass of air at altitude.
  • Pressure. Long, narrow or many-branched air lines can lose more than the allowance. Check valves and gang valves add drop.
  • Plants. This page is for fish tanks. Hydroponic reservoirs have different oxygen demand.
