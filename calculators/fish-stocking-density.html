How the stocking density calculator works

Stocking density is the weight of fish in a tank divided by the volume of water they live in. The calculator multiplies the number of fish by their average weight to get the biomass, then divides by the tank volume. Each input has its own unit; everything is converted to kilograms and liters before the math. The result is compared with a target maximum density to show how full the tank is, how many fish of that size it could hold, and how much more fish weight it can take before reaching the target.

Density is quoted per volume of fish-tank water. In metric units, kg/m³ and kg per 1,000 L are the same number, and grams per liter is the same number too.

Why stocking density matters

Every kilogram of fish uses oxygen and, through the feed it eats, adds ammonia, carbon dioxide and solid waste to the water. The more fish weight per liter, the faster water quality can crash if a pump, air blower or biofilter fails. In aquaponics the fish also have to match the plants: too few fish and the plants starve, too many and the biofilter and plants cannot keep up. Stocking density is the quick check growers use to stay inside what their aeration and filtration can handle.

Formulas

Biomass B (kg) = N × w Density D (kg/m³) = B ÷ V(m³) = B (kg) ÷ V (L) × 1000 Share of target (%) = D ÷ D_target × 100 Max biomass (kg) = D_target × V(m³) Max fish = floor(Max biomass ÷ w) Headroom (kg) = Max biomass − B

N is the number of fish, w the average weight of one fish in kg, V the water volume. Density in lb per US gallon is D ÷ 119.826 (1 lb/gal = 0.45359237 kg ÷ 3.785411784 L = 119.826 kg/m³).

Target densities (rules of thumb)

There is no single safe density. The figures below are published guidelines or examples, not limits that guarantee healthy fish. Safe density depends on aeration and oxygen supply, filtration (mechanical and biological), feeding rate, temperature, species and fish size, and on how quickly you can react to a failure.

For other species (trout, catfish, perch, carp and so on) enter your own target. Recommended maximums vary widely between species, life stages and sources, and research on rainbow trout shows that density alone is a poor measure of welfare; water quality, especially oxygen, matters more [4].

Example

A 1,000 L fish tank holds 40 tilapia averaging 400 g. Biomass is 40 × 0.4 kg = 16 kg. Density is 16 kg ÷ 1 m³ = 16 kg/m³ (16 kg per 1,000 L, or 0.134 lb/US gal). Against the FAO beginner limit of 20 kg/m³ the tank is at 80% of target. At 20 kg/m³ the tank can hold 20 kg, which is 20 ÷ 0.4 = 50 fish of this size, leaving 4 kg of headroom (10 more fish). As the fish grow, the same 40 fish reach the limit at 500 g each, so plan harvests or splits before then.

Limitations and assumptions

  • Fish grow. Density rises every day the fish eat. Check against the weight you expect at harvest, not only today's weight.
  • Average weight. Weigh a sample of fish (in water, in a bucket on a scale) and divide by the count. Guesses are often far off.
  • Tank water only. Use the volume of the fish tank. Water in sumps, grow beds and filters helps dilute waste but does not give the fish more space.
  • Small fish. Fingerlings are usually kept at lower kg/m³ than market-size fish, even though they are more numerous.
  • Feeding rate drives water quality. In aquaponics the FAO also sizes systems by daily feed per square meter of grow bed; density is a check on fish welfare, not on plant nutrient balance [1].
