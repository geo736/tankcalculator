How the foam fractionator calculator works

A foam fractionator (in the aquarium trade, a protein skimmer) is a column of water with a stream of fine air bubbles rising through it. Proteins, fats and other surface-active organic molecules in the water stick to the air–water surface of each bubble, and very fine particles and bacteria stick to the bubbles too. At the top the bubbles build up into a stable foam that rises up a narrow riser, spills into a collection cup and drains away as a small stream of dirty liquid (the foamate). In the usual counter-current design, water enters near the top and leaves at the bottom, so it flows down against the rising bubbles.

The calculator does what a designer does on paper:

  1. finds the water flow through the fractionator from turnovers of the system volume, a share of the main system flow, or the flow you enter, and splits it between the units;
  2. multiplies that flow by the contact time (hydraulic retention time) to get the column water volume;
  3. turns the volume into a column diameter and height, either from the water depth you can fit, or from a hydraulic loading (flow per m² of column), or checks a column you already have;
  4. multiplies the water flow by the air-to-water ratio to get the air flow, and for air stones at the base works out the pressure the air pump must supply at that depth and picks a pump from the same presets as the Air Pump & Stones: Fish calculator;
  5. reports how many times a day the system water passes through the skimmer, the solids the feed produces, and the ozone generator output for the dose you choose.

Why it matters

Screens and settlers remove the larger solids. What they miss are the very fine particles and the dissolved organic matter that build up in recirculating water, cloud it, feed heterotrophic bacteria, use oxygen and stress the fish. A foam fractionator targets exactly that fraction. Chen and co-workers at Cornell showed that foam fractionation removes both suspended and dissolved solids, including protein, from fish culture water (Weeks et al., 1992; Chen et al., 1993), and Timmons (1994) set out the design approach for aquaculture. In a freshwater trout RAS, Gregersen et al. (2021) found that foam fractionation alone cut particle numbers by about 58%, turbidity by 62%, bacterial activity by 54% and total BOD₅ by 51%. Removing fine solids also improves UV transmittance, which helps a UV sterilizer work.

The column is also a gas exchanger. The air bubbles add oxygen when the water is below saturation and strip some carbon dioxide, which is a useful side effect but not a replacement for proper aeration or a CO₂ stripper. The column is also a convenient, enclosed place to add ozone (Summerfelt, 2003).

Salt water versus fresh water

Foam fractionation works best in seawater. Dissolved salts slow down the merging (coalescence) of bubbles, so seawater keeps many small bubbles with a large total surface, and its foam is more stable (Craig, Ninham & Pashley, 1993). In fresh water, bubbles merge into fewer, larger ones that carry less surface and rise faster, and the foam collapses more easily, so less material is carried out. This is why foam fractionation is often seen as ineffective in fresh water (Gregersen et al., 2021). It does still work: in fresh water, Jafari et al. (2022) measured a 59% reduction in microparticles and 28% in turbidity, and adding salt to 10 ppt roughly doubled the reduction compared with 0 ppt.

Designers compensate in fresh and low-salinity water in three ways: a longer contact time (a bigger column for the same flow), more air per litre of water, and ozone. Ozone combined with foam fractionation gave the largest improvement in the Gregersen et al. (2021) trial: 89% fewer particles, 79% lower turbidity, 90% lower bacterial activity and 75% lower total BOD₅ than the control. More air is not always better, though: in seawater Barrut et al. (2013) found that raising the air flow reduced foam fractionation efficiency when feed was present, because the bubbles started to merge. The fresh-water defaults on this page (twice the contact time and twice the air ratio of the salt-water defaults) are rules of thumb, not published values; we did not find a published fresh-water design figure.

Formulas

1. Water flow Q through all fractionators, and per unit for N units:

From turnovers: Q = Vsystem × turnovers per hour From system flow: Q = Qsystem × share ÷ 100 Per unit: Qu = Q ÷ N

2. Column water volume for contact (hydraulic retention) time t:

V = Qu × t

3. Column cross-section, diameter and depth. The hydraulic loading HL is the water flow per m² of column cross-section A, which is also the speed at which the water moves down the column. Volume, area and depth H are tied together, so any two fix the third:

A = V ÷ H (from water depth) A = Qu ÷ HL (from hydraulic loading) H = V ÷ A = HL × t HL = Qu ÷ A = H ÷ t D = √(4A ÷ π) Total height ≈ H + riser and collection cup

To check an existing column of diameter D and water depth H: A = πD²/4, V = A × H and the contact time it gives is t = V ÷ Qu.

4. Air flow, with the air-to-water ratio G:L (air volume flow ÷ water volume flow):

Qair = G:L × Q (L/min, CFM = ft³/min, m³/h)

5. Air pressure for air stones or a diffuser at depth hd (gauge pressure at the pump, same method as the aeration calculator; ρw = 998 kg/m³ fresh to 1025 kg/m³ at 35 ppt, g = 9.80665 m/s²):

P = ρw × g × hd + diffuser drop + air line loss

The pump suggestion uses the linear air pump curves of the aeration calculator's presets. It picks the preset with the lowest total power that delivers the air at that pressure while keeping at least 15% of the pump's maximum pressure in reserve (air stones clog and the pressure rises). A venturi or injector pump draws its own air, so there is no air pump; check the injector's air-draw curve instead.

6. Turnover, solids and ozone, with daily feed F:

Passes per day = Q × 24 h ÷ Vsystem Solids (kg/day) = 0.25 × F (Timmons & Ebeling design value) Ozone (g/h) = dose (g O₃/kg feed) × F (kg/day) ÷ 24 Ozone per litre = ozone (g/h) ÷ Q (m³/h) (g/m³ = mg/L)

Contact time is not the whole story. Peng, Oh & Jo (2003) found that in a seawater foam fractionator the air rate per column area (superficial air velocity) was the dominant operating variable, and that the protein removal rate fell as the retention time increased. A longer column helps weak-foaming water, but enough fine air matters more.

Ozone dose

Ozone is dosed per kg of feed because the organic load it must oxidise comes from the feed. Published figures include 7–15 g O₃/kg feed to destroy the colour produced by the feed in a freshwater RAS (Christensen, Rusch & Malone, 2000), doses of 0, 7 and 14 g O₃/kg feed tested with full-scale skimmers in a commercial seawater RAS (Kovács et al., 2023), and about 29 g O₃/kg feed to disinfect the full flow, controlled by an ORP probe, in a freshwater RAS (Summerfelt et al., 2009). The default of 10 g/kg sits inside the colour range. Ozone and the oxidants it forms in seawater are toxic to fish: dose it into the fractionator or a contact chamber, never into the tank, control it by ORP and remove any residual before the water returns (Summerfelt, 2003).

Worked example (salt-water defaults)

A seawater system of 20 m³ at 35 ppt, fed 10 kg a day, sends its volume through one fractionator once an hour: Q = 20 × 1 = 20 m³/h (333.3 L/min).

Column volume for 2 min contact: 20 m³/h × 2/60 h = 0.667 m³ (667 L). With 1.5 m of water depth, A = 0.667 ÷ 1.5 = 0.444 m², so D = √(4 × 0.444 ÷ π) = 0.752 m (build 0.76 m). Hydraulic loading = 1.5 m ÷ (2/60 h) = 45 m/h (1.25 cm/s). Total height with a 0.5 m riser and cup: 2.0 m.

Air at 0.5 : 1: 0.5 × 20 = 10 m³/h = 166.7 L/min = 5.89 CFM. Air stones at 1.5 m in 1025 kg/m³ water: 1025 × 9.80665 × 1.5 = 15.08 kPa, plus 2 kPa diffuser and 1 kPa line = 18.08 kPa. The 120 L/min preset pump tops out at 17.9 kPa, so the suggestion is 4 × 60 L/min linear pumps (about 51 L/min each at that pressure, 200 W in total).

The system water passes through the skimmer 20 × 24 ÷ 20 = 24 times a day. The feed produces about 0.25 × 10 = 2.5 kg of solids a day. With ozone at 10 g/kg feed the generator must make 10 × 10 ÷ 24 = 4.17 g O₃/h, about 0.21 mg/L in the skimmer flow.

Worked example (fresh-water defaults)

Same system in fresh water (0 ppt), with the fresh-water starting values of 4 min and 1.0 : 1. Column volume = 20 × 4/60 = 1.333 m³; at 1.5 m depth A = 0.889 m² and D = 1.064 m (build 1.07 m); hydraulic loading 22.5 m/h. Air = 20 m³/h = 333.3 L/min = 11.77 CFM. Pressure = 998 × 9.80665 × 1.5 = 14.68 kPa + 3 kPa = 17.68 kPa. That is 98.6% of the 120 L/min pump's 17.9 kPa maximum, too close, so the page suggests 7 × 60 L/min pumps (about 52 L/min each, 350 W). The fresh-water column is twice the volume and uses twice the air of the seawater one for the same flow.

Limitations and assumptions

  • Rules of thumb, not a performance model. The calculator sizes the hardware; it does not predict how much will be removed. Removal depends on bubble size, salinity, the organic load, the air rate and the foam height, and published removal figures vary widely between systems.
  • Manufacturer data rules. Commercial skimmers are rated by their makers for a water flow, air flow and pump. Use that rating, and the injector or pump curves, for a purchase.
  • Plug flow. Contact time is the column water volume ÷ flow. Short-circuiting, the volume taken up by bubbles and a cone or bottom fittings reduce the real time.
  • Air volume is taken at the pump (about atmospheric pressure). Bubbles are compressed slightly at depth.
  • Venturi units are not pressure-checked: the injector pump draws air by suction, and the air it draws depends on its model and back-pressure.
  • Foamate (waste water leaving with the foam) is not estimated: we did not find a published typical fraction to base it on. It must be replaced with make-up water and disposed of like other sludge.
  • Ozone sizing assumes all of the generator output is transferred. Real transfer efficiency is below 100%, so allow extra capacity and use an ORP controller.
