How the mineralization tank calculator works

Fish turn part of their feed into solid waste (faeces and uneaten feed). A clarifier, swirl separator or drum filter takes much of it out of the water as a wet sludge. In a mineralization tank that sludge is mixed with water and aerated, and bacteria break the organic matter down. Nutrients such as phosphorus, potassium, calcium and micronutrients are released into the water in forms plants can take up, and that nutrient-rich water (the supernatant) is returned to the plants.

The calculator follows the sludge through four steps: how much dry solids the fish produce, how much of it your filter captures, how much wet sludge that makes at your sludge concentration, and how big a tank holds that sludge for the retention time you choose. Each input with a unit has its own unit picker; everything is converted to kilograms, liters and days before the calculation.

Why it matters in aquaponics

Fish feed is the only nutrient input in most aquaponic systems, and a large share of the phosphorus and other minerals in it ends up in the solids rather than dissolved in the water. Throwing the sludge away throws those nutrients away. Studies of aerobic sludge treatment for aquaponics found that aeration released phosphorus and potassium into solution, which makes the supernatant a useful supplement for plants (Monsees et al., 2017; Delaide et al., 2018; Delaide et al., 2019). Decoupled and "multi-loop" aquaponic designs build a mineralization step into the system for this reason (Goddek et al., 2019), and the FAO small-scale aquaponics manual describes letting captured solids mineralize in a separate aerated container before returning the water to the system (Somerville et al., 2014).

Formulas

Dry solids produced = Feed × solids fraction Dry solids captured = Dry solids produced × capture efficiency Sludge volume / day = Dry solids captured ÷ sludge concentration Working volume = Sludge volume / day × retention time Total tank volume = Working volume ÷ (1 − freeboard)

Sludge concentration is dry solids per volume of sludge. For dilute sludge (well under 10% solids) the density is close to that of water, so "% solids" by weight and "% w/v" are practically the same: 1% ≈ 10 g/L = 10 kg/m³. Freeboard is the share of the tank depth left empty above the liquid, so a 15% freeboard means the liquid fills 85% of the tank.

The tank is treated as fed with fresh sludge once a day while the same volume of clear supernatant leaves (or is decanted after the aeration is switched off and the solids settle), so the liquid stays in the tank for the retention time on average. The "dry solids loaded per retention period" line is simply the captured solids per day × retention time: the most the tank would ever hold if nothing were digested. In practice part of it is broken down, part is wasted as stable residue, and part leaves with the supernatant.

Worked example (the default inputs)

A system fed 1 kg of feed per day. With 25% of the feed turning into dry solids, the fish produce 1 × 0.25 = 0.25 kg of dry solids a day. The clarifier captures 80% of it: 0.25 × 0.80 = 0.2 kg dry solids per day. At 1% solids (10 kg/m³) that is 0.2 ÷ 10 = 0.02 m³ = 20 L of sludge per day. Holding it for 20 days needs 20 × 20 = 400 L of working volume. With 15% freeboard the tank should hold 400 ÷ 0.85 = 470.6 L in total, so a 500 L tank would fit. Over the 20 days the tank receives 0.2 × 20 = 4 kg of dry solids.

Notice how much the sludge concentration matters: the same solids at 0.2% (2 g/L, a much more dilute sludge) would be 100 L a day and need a 2,000 L working volume. Thickening the sludge first (letting it settle and pouring off clear water) shrinks the tank a lot.

Operating notes

  • Keep it aerobic. The tank is aerated continuously with an air pump and diffusers so that the bacteria have oxygen and the tank does not turn anaerobic and smelly. Aeration also lowers the pH as the solids are oxidised, which helps dissolve phosphorus and other minerals (Monsees et al., 2017). This page does not size the air pump; no simple, well-sourced rule exists for this use.
  • Batch or continuous. Delaide et al. (2019) describe batch operation: stop the air, let the solids settle, draw off the supernatant for the plants, and refill with fresh sludge and water. A tank fed little and often behaves like the continuous case used here.
  • Watch the plants' water. The supernatant can be rich in sodium and chloride from feed and may need dilution (Rakocy et al., 2007).

Limitations

  • The solids fraction is a rule of thumb. High-energy, highly digestible feeds produce less solid waste; poor feeding practice (uneaten feed) produces more.
  • The calculator ignores the volume reduction from digestion and the residue that never breaks down; both mean a real tank slowly accumulates inert solids that must be removed from time to time.
  • Mineralization speed depends on temperature, pH, aeration and solids content. The 5–30 day range is what has been reported, not a guarantee; check the supernatant (EC, phosphate) to tune the retention time for your system.
  • Anaerobic digesters work differently (slower, produce biogas) and are not covered here.
