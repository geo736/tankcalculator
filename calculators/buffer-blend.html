Aquaponic Buffer Blend Calculator

Fish waste brings plenty of nitrogen but too little potassium, calcium and magnesium for good plant growth. Design a dry blend of base and mineral salts that you add in proportion to the fish feed, so the system water reaches a hydroponic nutrient ratio and the bases in it replace the alkalinity that nitrification uses up. Based on Wilson Lennard's buffer formulation model.

    How the buffer blend calculator works

    This calculator is built on the Aquaponic System Sizing & Buffer Formulation Model by Wilson Lennard (PhD) [1]. The idea is his: the fish feed is the system's nutrient input, so the nutrients the plants are short of can be added in proportion to the feed, as one dry blend of salts. Base salts (calcium hydroxide, potassium carbonate) in the same blend also replace the alkalinity that nitrifying bacteria use up, so the blend doubles as the system's pH buffer.

    1. Nitrogen from the feed. Feed protein ÷ 6.25 is the feed's nitrogen. The fish keep part of it in their growth; the rest is waste that the biofilter turns into nitrate for the plants.
    2. Phosphorus from the feed, likewise: feed P minus the P kept in the fish.
    3. Solids. If the fish solids are captured and remineralised (digested and returned), all the waste counts. If they are removed, the N and P in them are taken off.
    4. Targets. A hydroponic reference solution gives the ratio of each nutrient to nitrogen. Multiplying the plant-available N by those ratios gives how much P, K, Ca, Mg and S the plants need per kg of feed.
    5. The blend. What the fish waste (and, optionally, the make-up water) does not supply is made up with the salts you tick. The calculator finds the salt amounts with a non-negative least-squares solver [10]. When the salts can match every target exactly, this gives the same answer as the spreadsheet's step-by-step sequence; when they cannot, it finds the closest fit and shows what is over or under.
    6. Checks. It reports the ratios produced, the chloride added, the alkalinity balance, and how many plants of each crop the plant-available N can feed at your feed rate.

    Why it matters

    Fish feed is formulated for fish, not plants. Aquaponic water usually holds enough nitrate, phosphate and sulfate for good plant growth, but too little potassium and calcium, and sometimes too little magnesium; iron is also short [7, 8]. Fish need little potassium, so feeds contain little of it [11]. At the same time nitrification acidifies the water. The University of the Virgin Islands system solves both problems at once by adding potassium hydroxide and calcium hydroxide in equal amounts, alternately, several times a week to hold pH near 7 [7]. Lennard's model takes that practice one step further: instead of letting pH decide how much K and Ca go in, it sizes each salt to a plant nutrient ratio and ties the dose to the feed.

    Formulas

    Per kg of fish weight gain, with protein and P in % of the feed, FCR in kg feed per kg fish gain, and Nret, Pret the N and P kept in the fish (g/kg) [1, 3]:

    N fed = protein × 10 × FCR ÷ 6.25 (g N per kg fish) N waste = N fed − Nret P waste = P% × 10 × FCR − Pret (g P per kg fish) per kg feed = per kg fish ÷ FCR

    With solids removed, sN and sP = share of the feed N and P that leaves in the solids:

    N available = N waste per kg feed − sN × protein × 10 ÷ 6.25 P available = P waste per kg feed − sP × P% × 10

    Targets per kg of feed from the reference solution (all as elements, mg/L):

    targetX = N available × refX ÷ refN X = P, K, Ca, Mg, S neededX = targetX − fish wasteX − make-up waterX waterX = CX (mg/L) × water (L/day) ÷ 1000 ÷ feed (kg/day)

    Element share of each salt, from IUPAC atomic weights [9] (n = atoms per formula unit, M = molar mass with water of crystallisation):

    fraction = n × A ÷ M × purity Ca(OH)₂ 74.09: Ca 54.1% K₂CO₃ 138.20: K 56.6% KH₂PO₄ 136.08: K 28.7%, P 22.8% MgSO₄·7H₂O 246.47: Mg 9.86%, S 13.0% MgCl₂·6H₂O 203.30: Mg 12.0%, Cl 34.9% K₂SO₄ 174.25: K 44.9%, S 18.4% KHCO₃ 100.11: K 39.1% KOH 56.11: K 69.7% MgCl₂ 95.21: Mg 25.5% CaCl₂·2H₂O 147.01: Ca 27.3%, Cl 48.2% CaCO₃ 100.09: Ca 40.0% CaMg(CO₃)₂ 184.40: Ca 21.7%, Mg 13.2%

    The solver picks salt amounts x ≥ 0 that minimise the squared relative error over P, K, Ca, Mg and S, each scaled by its target (the same Lawson–Hanson method as the nutrient additions calculator). A very small penalty on chloride makes it prefer chloride-free salts when two combinations fit equally well.

    Alkalinity, as g CaCO₃ per kg feed (1 equivalent = 50.04 g CaCO₃):

    supplied = Σ salt (g) × purity ÷ M × equivalents Ca(OH)₂, K₂CO₃, CaCO₃: 2 CaMg(CO₃)₂: 4 KOH, KHCO₃: 1 nitrification = N available × 7.14 (7.05 with bacterial growth [6]) plant NO₃ uptake returns up to N × 50.04 ÷ 14.007 = 3.57 g per g N

    Plants supported (the spreadsheet's crop table):

    plants = N available (g/kg feed) × feed (kg/day) ÷ N use (g N/plant/day)

    Worked example (the defaults)

    32% protein feed with 0.8% P, FCR 1.5, 1 kg of feed a day, all solids remineralised, Resh reference read as elements, and the spreadsheet's five salts plus potassium sulfate ticked.

    N fed = 32 × 10 × 1.5 ÷ 6.25 = 76.8 g/kg fish; waste = 76.8 − 28.8 = 48.0; ÷ 1.5 = 32.0 g N per kg feed P waste = 0.8 × 10 × 1.5 − 4.1 = 7.9 g/kg fish; ÷ 1.5 = 5.267 g P per kg feed Targets (× ref ÷ 121): P 9.521, K 63.47, Ca 23.01, Mg 16.13, S 25.92 g per kg feed KH₂PO₄ = (9.521 − 5.267) × 136.084 ÷ 30.974 = 18.69 g (brings 5.37 g K) Ca(OH)₂ = 23.01 × 74.092 ÷ 40.078 = 42.54 g K₂CO₃ = (63.47 − 5.37) × 138.204 ÷ 78.196 = 102.69 g

    That leaves magnesium and sulfur. Taking all 25.92 g S from magnesium sulfate would bring 25.92 × 24.305 ÷ 32.06 = 19.65 g Mg against a target of 16.13 g, so part of the sulfur must come from a salt without magnesium. With potassium sulfate ticked, the solver takes all the Mg from 163.59 g MgSO₄·7H₂O (16.13 g Mg, 21.28 g S) and the remaining 4.64 g S from 25.21 g K₂SO₄, whose 11.31 g K lowers potassium carbonate to 82.70 g. Magnesium chloride is not needed. Every target is met exactly. The blend is 332.7 g per kg of feed: 12.8% Ca(OH)₂, 24.9% K₂CO₃, 49.2% MgSO₄·7H₂O, 7.6% K₂SO₄ and 5.6% KH₂PO₄. It supplies 117.3 g CaCO₃-equivalent of alkalinity per kg feed against 32.0 × 7.14 = 228.5 g used by nitrification (51%); plant nitrate uptake may return up to 114 g of the difference, so measure pH and top up with base if needed. The plant-available N is 32 g per day, enough for about 2,000 large lettuces or 213 tomato plants at the model's N-use values.

    With only the spreadsheet's five salts (untick potassium sulfate), sulfur and magnesium cannot both be met. The solver settles on 177.95 g MgSO₄·7H₂O, which gives S 23.15 g (−10.7%) and Mg 17.55 g (+8.8%), with 102.69 g K₂CO₃ and no MgCl₂: a 341.9 g blend.

    Corrections to the original spreadsheet

    The spreadsheet's method is sound, and the page follows it. Checking it line by line turned up a few slips, which the page corrects:

    1. Element versus ion basis. Resh's reference values are elements: nitrogen as NO₃-N, phosphorus as P and sulfur as S [2]. The spreadsheet converted the fish-waste N to nitrate ion (× 62/14) and P to phosphate ion (× 95/31) before taking ratios against them, which treats the reference values as ions. That inflates K, Ca and Mg 4.4-fold relative to N (and P and S about 1.45-fold): 1,164 g of salts per kg feed instead of about 340 g. The page reads the values as elements. The "ions" setting is kept only so the spreadsheet can be reproduced, and is not recommended.
    2. Magnesium sulfate molar mass. MgSO₄·7H₂O is 246.47 g/mol, not 236; the page uses IUPAC atomic weights for every salt [9].
    3. Solid N per kg of feed. The "solid N waste (g/kg fish feed)" cell takes 10% of the N fed per kg of fish (7.68 g); per kg of feed it is 10% of 51.2 g = 5.12 g. The page also makes solids handling an explicit choice.
    4. Swapped labels. In the dry-blend composition, the labels "Monopotassium phosphate" and "Magnesium chloride" are swapped: 14.6% is magnesium chloride and 3.2% monopotassium phosphate.
    5. Magnesium chloride form. The sheet uses anhydrous MgCl₂ (95 g/mol). Most agricultural magnesium chloride is the hexahydrate (203.30 g/mol), which takes 2.1 times the weight for the same Mg; both are offered, and the chloride added is reported.
    6. Exact equality in the ratio check. The sheet flags SO₄:K as "No" only because of floating-point rounding; the page compares ratios within a tolerance.
    7. Infeasible targets. A fixed sequence can call for a negative amount, as magnesium chloride does with the element reading (see the example). The page uses a non-negative solver over the ticked salts, offers potassium sulfate and other salts so S and Mg can be met separately, and shows what ends up over or under.

    Reproduction check: with the ions setting and the sheet's simplified molar masses, an independent hand calculation gives exactly the spreadsheet's 188.50 g Ca(OH)₂, 478.41 g K₂CO₃, 282.16 g MgSO₄·7H₂O, 169.21 g MgCl₂ and 37.25 g KH₂PO₄ per kg feed. With IUPAC masses and anhydrous MgCl₂ the page gives 188.29, 477.66, 294.37, 166.02 and 37.25 g.

    Which salts can share a container

    The calculator groups the salts it uses into containers. Salts that do not react with each other are mixed into one blend, so they can be weighed and dosed together. The others get their own container:

    • Base blend: calcium hydroxide, potassium carbonate and potassium bicarbonate. These are all alkaline and can share a container if it is kept dry and sealed. With moisture, calcium hydroxide reacts with the carbonates to form calcium carbonate and caustic potassium hydroxide, and the blend cakes. An equal mixture of potassium bicarbonate and calcium hydroxide has been used as a single buffer in a research aquaponic system, with good lettuce yields [15].
    • Mineral blend: monopotassium phosphate, potassium sulfate and magnesium sulfate. These are neutral salts with no calcium, so they can be mixed and dissolved together. They are kept apart from the base blend because calcium with phosphate forms insoluble calcium phosphate [7], and because the water of crystallisation in Epsom salt would let the bases turn the magnesium into insoluble magnesium hydroxide.
    • Slow-release carbonates: calcium carbonate (limestone) and dolomite. Both are used to add calcium (and, from dolomite, magnesium) and alkalinity to aquaponic systems [14, 16]. They barely dissolve at neutral pH and dissolve faster as nitrification makes the water more acidic, so they act as a self-limiting buffer rather than a dose. Their low solubility also means the calcium arrives slowly: in one long-term system, daily lime additions still left the tomatoes short of calcium and magnesium [17]. Place them in a mesh bag, media bed or reactor in the water flow, and do not count on them alone to meet a calcium target.
    • Kept separate: potassium hydroxide (very hygroscopic and caustic, and it heats as it dissolves); magnesium chloride and calcium chloride (they absorb moisture and cake a blend, and the calcium in calcium chloride would react with the phosphate and sulfate).

    These are handling rules based on the chemistry of the salts. Always add each container's salts slowly and check pH.

    Limitations and assumptions

    • Fish waste minerals are not counted. Fish feed also contains K, Ca, Mg and S, part of which reaches the water. The model counts only its N and P, so the blend may slightly oversupply these elements. Enter hard make-up water under "Minerals in make-up water"; in one study more than 75% of the Ca, Mg and S came from tap water [12].
    • No iron or micronutrients. Aquaponic water is usually short of iron; add it separately as a chelate (see the iron addition calculator). Manganese and other micronutrients can also run short.
    • No sodium is modelled; avoid sodium bicarbonate as a buffer in aquaponics, as sodium builds up.
    • Ratios, not concentrations. The blend sets the proportions of nutrients entering the system. The concentrations the water reaches depend on plant uptake, water exchange, solids removal and denitrification. Measure EC and, every few weeks, the main ions, and adjust.
    • No ion interactions. Calcium from the blend can precipitate phosphate as calcium phosphate, especially at higher pH [7]; nothing here models precipitation or uptake.
    • Plant N use per plant are the Lennard model's figures, not independently verified. As a rough check for lettuce, a 200 g head at about 4.4% dry matter and 4.9% N in the dry matter holds about 0.43 g N, or about 14 mg per day over 30 days. Fruiting crops vary with stage and yield.
    • Alkalinity is a guide. Nitrification uses about 7.1 g CaCO₃ per g N, but plant nitrate uptake gives some back and denitrification in anoxic zones gives more. Whether the blend's base is enough depends on your system; measure pH and alkalinity and adjust with the pH Up calculator.
    • Phosphate salt acidity. KH₂PO₄ is counted as neutral; near pH 7 it releases a little acid as it turns into HPO₄²⁻.
    • Several salts with the same nutrients (for example K₂CO₃ and KOH, or Ca(OH)₂ and CaCO₃) are interchangeable for the fit; the solver then uses one of them (or a mix). Untick the ones you do not want to use.

    Safety

    • Calcium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide are caustic, and potassium carbonate is strongly alkaline. They burn skin and eyes. Wear goggles, gloves and a dust mask when weighing and mixing.
    • Dissolving KOH releases a lot of heat: add it slowly to cold water, never water to the solid.
    • Dose slowly into a well-mixed sump or biofilter outlet, away from the fish, and check pH before the next dose. Fish and nitrifying bacteria cope better with gradual change [8].
    • Keep each container dry and sealed, and keep the containers apart as shown under "How to mix it": the base blend, the mineral blend, the carbonates and any separate salts.
    • Label the containers and store them away from acids, children and animals.
