How the pump and turnover calculator works

Turnover is how many times the whole volume of water in the system passes through the pump (and so through the filters and grow beds) each hour. If a system holds 1,000 L and the pump moves 2,000 L every hour, the water turns over twice per hour, or once every 30 minutes.

In Pump flow needed mode the calculator multiplies your system volume by the turnovers per hour you want. In Turnover of my pump mode it does the reverse: it divides the pump's flow by the system volume. Both modes then work out the power needed to lift that flow to your total head, the electrical power the pump is likely to draw, and what it costs to run.

Why turnover matters

In aquaponics and recirculating aquaculture (RAS), the pump is what carries fish waste to the filters and nitrifying bacteria, brings treated water back to the fish, and delivers nutrients to the plants. Too little flow lets ammonia and solids build up in the fish tank; too much wastes electricity and can stress fish or wash solids past the filters. In hydroponics the same sum tells you how often the nutrient reservoir circulates through the channels or beds.

The pump usually runs 24 hours a day, so it is often the single biggest electricity user in a small system. Choosing a pump that is just big enough at your real head saves money every month.

Turnover guidance (rules of thumb)

There is no single correct turnover rate. It depends on stocking density, feeding rate, filter design and the plants. Published guidance is a starting point:

Turnovers per hourTypical use
below 1×Slow. Below the common aquaponics minimum, but can be fine for lightly stocked systems or plant-only reservoirs. The UVI commercial aquaponic system ran its rearing tanks at an average retention time of 82 minutes (about 0.7× per hour) [3].
1–2×Typical small aquaponics. About once per hour is widely used for light stocking; the FAO manual recommends cycling the water about twice per hour in densely stocked units, e.g. 2,000 L/h for 1,000 L of water [1].
2–6×Above typical aquaponics guidance; more common in intensive RAS culture tanks [2].
above 6×Very fast. Small intensive tanks can be exchanged as often as every 10 minutes [2], but check fish comfort, solids capture and pumping cost.

These bands are rules of thumb, not hard limits. The result list shows which band your number falls in.

Formulas

Flow and turnover, with V the total system volume and N the turnovers per hour:

Q = V × N (e.g. L × 1/h = L/h) N = Q ÷ V Turnover time = 60 min ÷ N

Hydraulic power is the rate at which the pump adds energy to the water. It is the standard pump power relation from fluid mechanics [4]:

Phyd = ρ × g × Q × H ρ = 998.2 kg/m³ (water at 20 °C) g = 9.80665 m/s² (standard gravity) Q = flow in m³/s, H = total head in m

Electrical power: real pumps lose energy in the motor, impeller and volute. The wire-to-water efficiency η is the fraction of electrical power that ends up as hydraulic power [4]:

Pelec = Phyd ÷ η

If you enter the pump's power from its label or a plug-in meter, the calculator uses that number instead.

Energy and cost, with t the hours of running per day:

Energy per day = Pelec (kW) × t Energy per month = Energy per day × 30.44 (365.25 ÷ 12) Cost = Energy (kWh) × price per kWh

Example

The default inputs follow the FAO small-scale unit example: 1,000 L of water, 2 turnovers per hour and 1.5 m of head [1].

  • Flow: Q = 1,000 L × 2 /h = 2,000 L/h (0.000556 m³/s, about 528 US gph or 8.8 US gpm). Turnover time = 60 ÷ 2 = 30 min.
  • Hydraulic power: 998.2 × 9.80665 × 0.000556 × 1.5 = 8.16 W.
  • At 25% efficiency the pump draws about 8.16 ÷ 0.25 = 32.6 W.
  • Running 24 h a day: 32.6 W × 24 h = 0.783 kWh/day, or 23.8 kWh per month. At 0.15 per kWh that is about 3.57 per month.

Pump curves: the flow on the box is not the flow you get

Pump boxes usually show the maximum flow at zero head. Every metre of lift and every metre of pipe, elbow and valve reduces the flow. At the pump's "max head" the flow falls to zero. Always look up the manufacturer's pump curve (flow against head), find your total head on it, and check that the flow there is at least the required flow from this calculator. In Turnover of my pump mode, enter the flow read from the curve at your head.

Things the calculator does not include

  • Friction losses. The calculator uses the total head you enter. Long runs of narrow pipe and many fittings can add a lot of head; include an allowance for them.
  • Efficiency. The 15–35% hint for small submersible pumps is a rough rule of thumb, and efficiency changes along the pump curve. A plug-in power meter gives the real number.
  • Air-lifts, timers and siphons. Flood-and-drain beds on timers, bell siphons and air-lift pumps change how flow and energy relate. Set the run time to the hours the pump actually runs.
  • Water density. Uses freshwater at 20 °C. Seawater (about 1,025 kg/m³) needs about 3% more power.
