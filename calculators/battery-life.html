How the battery life estimator works

The calculator works out how much energy the battery bank can safely give, how much power your critical equipment draws on average, and divides one by the other. In steps:

  1. Bank energy. Each battery stores roughly its capacity in amp-hours (Ah) times its nominal voltage, in watt-hours (Wh) (Reddy, 2011). The batteries are added together. Wiring them in series raises the voltage and wiring them in parallel raises the amp-hours, but the total energy is the same either way.
  2. Usable energy. You should not drain a battery completely. The usable depth of discharge (DoD) is the share of the capacity you plan to use before the inverter or controller cuts off. A second percentage lets you derate the battery for age or cold.
  3. Average load. For each device, power × quantity × duty cycle. A pump on a timer that runs 15 minutes per hour has a 25% duty cycle.
  4. Battery draw. AC equipment runs through an inverter that wastes some energy as heat, so the battery supplies the AC load divided by the inverter efficiency, plus the inverter’s own no-load draw.
  5. Peukert correction (lead-acid). Lead-acid batteries give fewer amp-hours when they are discharged faster than the rate their capacity is rated at, usually the 20-hour rate. The calculator reduces the usable energy by Peukert’s law when the current per battery is higher than that rated current.
  6. Runtime = usable energy ÷ battery draw. The calculator also tries larger banks until the runtime reaches your target.

Why backup runtime matters

In recirculating aquaculture, aquaponics and hydroponics, pumps and blowers keep oxygen in the water and water moving through filters and roots. When the power goes out, dissolved oxygen in a heavily stocked fish tank can fall to dangerous levels quickly, and deep-water or NFT plant roots also lose their oxygen and flow. The SRAC fact sheet on managing recirculating systems states that backup power is essential (Masser et al., 1999). A generator with an automatic transfer switch is the usual first line of defence. A battery bank with an inverter, or a DC air pump on its own battery, is a quiet and simple way to keep aeration going during short outages or until the generator starts. This calculator tells you whether the bank you have is enough.

Formulas

N = number of batteries, C = rated capacity per battery (Ah), V = nominal voltage per battery, DoD = usable depth of discharge, A = capacity available (age/cold derating), η = inverter efficiency (1 for DC loads).

Nominal energy E_nom = N × C × V Usable energy E_use = E_nom × DoD × A Average AC load P_avg = Σ (P_i × qty_i × duty_i) Battery draw P_bat = P_avg ÷ η + P_no-load Current/battery I = P_bat ÷ (N × V) Runtime t = E_use × f ÷ P_bat

Every battery carries the same current whatever the wiring: in series the whole bank current flows through each battery, and in parallel the larger bank current is shared equally between the batteries. That is why the current per battery is Pbat ÷ (N × V) in all cases.

Peukert factor. Peukert’s law (Peukert, 1897) says a battery rated at C amp-hours over H hours lasts t = H × (C ÷ (I × H))k hours at a constant current I. Dividing by the simple estimate C ÷ I gives the fraction of rated capacity you actually get:

f = (C ÷ (I × H))^(k − 1), capped at f ≤ 1

The cap is a conservative choice. At currents below the rated current the formula predicts more than the rated capacity, but real batteries gain less than that, so the calculator never credits extra capacity. Peukert’s law is only an approximation; the exponent is normally worked out from two rated discharge times, such as the 5-hour and 20-hour ratings on a datasheet (Victron Energy).

Battery University gives typical end-of-discharge voltages of 1.75 V per cell for lead-acid (10.5 V for a 12 V battery) and 3.0 V per cell for most lithium-ion; by that point about 95% of the energy is used. Going all the way to that voltage is a 100% discharge, and lead-acid batteries cycled that deeply last far fewer cycles. That is why 50% is the common rule of thumb for lead-acid backup banks. The k values are Victron’s suggested defaults when the real value is unknown: 1.25 for lead-acid and 1.05 for lithium.

Example

Two 12 V, 100 Ah AGM batteries in parallel power two 50 W linear air pumps running all the time and a 100 W water pump on a 50% timer, through an inverter with 85% efficiency (no-load draw ignored).

E_nom = 2 × 100 Ah × 12 V = 2,400 Wh E_use = 2,400 × 0.50 × 1.00 = 1,200 Wh P_avg = 2 × 50 W + 100 W × 0.50 = 150 W P_bat = 150 ÷ 0.85 = 176.5 W I = 176.5 ÷ (2 × 12) = 7.35 A per battery Rated current C ÷ H = 100 ÷ 20 = 5 A f = (5 ÷ 7.35)^(1.25 − 1) = 0.68^0.25 = 0.908 t = 1,200 × 0.908 ÷ 176.5 = 6.17 h ≈ 6 h 10 min

Without the Peukert correction the same bank would be estimated at 6.8 h, so the faster discharge costs about 40 minutes. For a 12-hour target the bank needs 4 batteries: with four in parallel the current falls to 3.7 A per battery, below the 20-hour rate, and the runtime is 2,400 Wh ÷ 176.5 W = 13.6 h.

Limitations and assumptions

  • Cold. Batteries deliver less in the cold. Battery University reports that a battery giving 100% at 27 °C (80 °F) typically delivers only about 50% at −18 °C (0 °F). Lower “Capacity available” for unheated sheds and winter outages.
  • Age. Capacity fades with use. IEEE Std 485 treats a lead-acid battery as at end of life when it can give only 80% of its rated capacity, so sizing practice adds about 25% to cover ageing. Use about 80% capacity available to plan for an old bank.
  • Voltage sag. The calculator uses nominal voltage. Under heavy load a lead-acid battery’s voltage drops, and the inverter may shut off at its low-voltage cutoff before the planned DoD is reached.
  • Start-up surge. Pumps and aerator motors draw several times their running power for a moment when they start. This barely changes runtime, but the inverter’s surge rating must cover it.
  • Inverter idle draw. An inverter uses power even with little load. With small air pumps this can be most of the drain, so enter the no-load figure from the datasheet.
  • Starting charge. The estimate assumes the batteries are fully charged when the outage starts. Keep the bank on a float or maintenance charger.
  • Peukert is approximate and assumes a constant current. Pumps cycling on and off are treated as their average current. Peukert’s law describes lead-acid well and lithium only loosely.
