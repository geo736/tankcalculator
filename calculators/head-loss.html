How the head loss calculator works

A pump has to do two jobs. It lifts water from the sump up to where it is discharged (the static lift), and it overcomes the friction of the water rubbing along the pipe wall and swirling through elbows, tees and valves (the friction and minor losses). Engineers express all three as a height of water column called head. Added together they give the total dynamic head (TDH), the number you look up on a pump curve together with your flow rate.

The calculator finds the water velocity from the flow and the pipe’s inside diameter, works out the pipe friction with either the Darcy-Weisbach or the Hazen-Williams equation, adds a loss coefficient K for every fitting, and adds the static lift. The visualization draws the pipe run and stacks the three parts of the TDH on the same vertical scale, so the static part of the bar lines up with the real lift.

Why it matters in aquaponics, aquaculture and hydroponics

Most recirculating systems run a pump 24 hours a day, so a pump that is too small fails to deliver the turnover the fish or plants need, and one that is too big wastes electricity for years. Pump manufacturers rate pumps by flow at a given head. A pump sold as “2,000 gph” may deliver less than half that against 10 ft of head. Knowing your TDH lets you pick the pump on its curve, and the breakdown shows where head is going: if fittings or pipe friction are a large share, a bigger pipe or fewer elbows is often cheaper than a bigger pump. Timmons & Ebeling (2010) treat pump and pipe sizing this way for recirculating aquaculture systems.

Velocity and Reynolds number

A = π D² / 4 V = Q / A Re = V D / ν (ν = μ / ρ, kinematic viscosity)

Flow is laminar below Re ≈ 2,000, turbulent above about 4,000, and unpredictable in between. Almost all pumped water lines in fish and plant systems are turbulent. Water viscosity changes a lot with temperature (it is about twice as viscous at 0 °C as at 30 °C), so the calculator uses density and viscosity at your water temperature, interpolated from IAPWS reference values between 0 and 80 °C.

Pipe friction: Darcy-Weisbach

hf = f × (L / D) × V² / (2g)

The Darcy friction factor f depends on Re and the relative roughness ε/D. In turbulent flow it is found from the Colebrook equation, which has f on both sides and is solved by iteration:

1/√f = −2 log₁₀( (ε/D)/3.7 + 2.51 / (Re √f) )

The calculator starts the iteration from the explicit Swamee-Jain (1976) approximation and also shows that value for comparison. Swamee-Jain is stated to be within about 1% of Colebrook for 10⁻⁶ ≤ ε/D ≤ 10⁻² and 5,000 ≤ Re ≤ 10⁸:

f = 0.25 / [ log₁₀( (ε/D)/3.7 + 5.74 / Re0.9 ) ]²

For laminar flow (Re < 2,000) f = 64 / Re. Darcy-Weisbach works for any temperature and pipe size, so it is the default.

Pipe friction: Hazen-Williams

V = 0.849 × C × Rh0.63 × S0.54 (SI units, Rh = D/4, S = hf/L) hf ≈ 10.67 × L × Q1.852 / (C1.852 × D4.87) (Q in m³/s, D and L in m)

Hazen-Williams is an empirical formula for water at ordinary temperatures. It is popular in irrigation and plumbing, but it ignores temperature and is usually quoted for pipes of about 2 in (50 mm) and larger and velocities below about 3 m/s (10 ft/s). The calculator uses the velocity form above; the second line is the common rearranged form and agrees within about 1%.

Fittings (minor losses)

hm = ΣK × V² / (2g) K = n × fT (Crane TP-410 method)

Crane Technical Paper 410 gives most fitting resistances as a multiple n of fT, the friction factor of clean commercial steel pipe of that size in fully turbulent flow. fT is about 0.026 for ½ in pipe, 0.020 for 1½ in and 0.016 for 4 in; the calculator interpolates Crane’s table using your inside diameter. FittingCraneK (1½ in)

Crane’s values are for threaded or welded steel fittings. Solvent-weld PVC fittings have similar shapes, so these are a reasonable estimate, but different manufacturers’ fittings can differ by a factor of two or more. A partly closed valve, a strainer, a UV unit or a filter adds much more loss: use the manufacturer’s pressure-drop figure and add it to the static lift.

Total dynamic head, pressure and power

TDH = static lift + hf + hm p = ρ g × TDH Phydraulic = ρ g Q × TDH

The hydraulic power is the power delivered to the water. The pump draws more electricity than this: divide by the pump-and-motor efficiency from the manufacturer (small submersible pumps are often well below 50% efficient).

Example

Default inputs: 20 US gpm through 50 ft of 1½ in Schedule 40 PVC (ID 1.610 in), 5 ft static lift, water at 77 °F (25 °C), with four 90° elbows, one tee (run), one ball valve, one swing check valve, one entrance and one exit.

  • Q = 20 gpm = 0.001262 m³/s; D = 0.04089 m; A = 0.001313 m²; V = 0.961 m/s (3.15 ft/s).
  • At 25 °C, ν = 0.890 × 10⁻³ / 997.05 = 0.893 mm²/s, so Re = 0.961 × 0.04089 / 0.893 × 10⁻⁶ ≈ 44,000 (turbulent).
  • ε/D = 0.0015 / 40.89 = 3.7 × 10⁻⁵; Colebrook gives f = 0.0216 (Swamee-Jain 0.0215).
  • Velocity head V²/2g = 0.0471 m. Pipe friction hf = 0.0216 × (15.24 / 0.04089) × 0.0471 = 0.379 m = 1.24 ft.
  • fT = 0.020, so ΣK = 4 × 0.60 + 0.40 + 0.06 + 2.00 + 0.5 + 1.0 = 6.36 and hm = 6.36 × 0.0471 = 0.299 m = 0.98 ft.
  • TDH = 5 + 1.24 + 0.98 = 7.23 ft (2.20 m, 3.12 psi). Hydraulic power = 997 × 9.807 × 0.001262 × 2.20 ≈ 27 W (0.036 hp).

The swing check valve alone (K = 2.0) costs as much head as about 12 ft of this pipe. With Hazen-Williams (C = 150) the pipe friction comes out at 1.23 ft instead.

Limitations and assumptions

  • One pipe size. The whole run is treated as a single diameter. For a suction line and a discharge line of different sizes, or for branches, calculate each section and add the losses.
  • Clean water. Viscosity is for fresh water. Seawater is about 8% more viscous; water with heavy solids or sludge behaves differently.
  • Fitting values are estimates. K values vary with manufacturer, size and how the fitting is made. Short-radius and sanitary fittings, reducers and partly open valves are not included.
  • Velocity guide is a rule of thumb. Keeping water near 0.3–1.5 m/s (1–5 ft/s) is common practice to limit friction, noise and water hammer while keeping solids moving. It is not a standard.
  • Static lift is measured from the sump water surface to the point of discharge (or to the water surface if the pipe discharges below it). A pressurized outlet, such as a spray bar or a pressurized filter, needs its pressure added as extra head.
  • Add a safety margin before choosing a pump, and check the pump’s net positive suction head (NPSH) if it sits above the water.
