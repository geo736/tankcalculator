How the fish hauling calculator works

Hatcheries and extension services publish loading rates: the weight of fish that can be hauled per gallon of water. The rate depends on the fish's size, the water temperature and how long the trip takes. Small fish use more oxygen per pound than large fish, warm water speeds up their metabolism, and on a long trip ammonia and carbon dioxide have more time to build up. So the safe rate goes down for smaller fish, warmer water and longer trips.

For channel catfish the calculator looks up the classic table from Fish Hatchery Management (Piper et al., 1982, based on McCraren's data). The same table is reprinted in Texas and North Central Region catfish culture guides. It interpolates between the table's fish sizes and trip lengths, then adjusts for water temperature. The result is multiplied by the water in your tank to give the maximum load. For any other species, enter the loading rate from a guide written for that species. The same temperature rule, displacement, oxygen, salt and tempering steps are then applied.

Why it matters

Hauling is one of the most stressful things that happens to farmed fish. According to SRAC Publication 390, dissolved oxygen and the build-up of wastes (ammonia and carbon dioxide) are the main limits on how densely fish can be hauled. Temperature, pH, loading density, fish condition and trip length all change those limits. Fish that are overloaded may look fine when they are unloaded and then die days later. This matters whether you are moving fingerlings into an aquaponics system, delivering stockers to ponds, or taking food-size fish to market. SRAC's own advice is to treat published rates as a starting point, be conservative, and only increase loads based on your own experience.

Formulas

Temperature correction. The catfish guides say loading rates may be increased by 25% for each 10 °F (5.5 °C) the water is cooler than 65 °F, and must be reduced by 25% for each 10 °F it is warmer. The calculator applies this linearly. It caps the increase at +50% (45 °F), because the guides do not go further.

fT = 1 + 0.25 × (65 − T°F) ÷ 10 (capped at 1.5) rate = rate65 °F(fish per lb, hours) × fT

Air only. The table assumes good aeration. The University of Kentucky catfish hauling guide (Wynne & Wurts) gives about 4 lb of food-size catfish per gallon as a general rule when the water is aerated with air (21% oxygen) instead of pure oxygen. When you choose Air only, the catfish rate is capped at 4 lb/gal × fT. This cap only changes the result for large fish. With small fish and air only, it is wise to haul less than the table allows.

Maximum load and number of fish.

Max load (lb) = rate (lb/gal) × water (gal) Max fish = Max load ÷ weight per fish

Water and tank space. The rate is per gallon of water before the fish go in. Water pushed up by the fish does not count. SRAC 393 gives the rule of thumb that about 8 lb of fish displace about 1 gallon, or 12 gallons per 100 lb of fish (about 1.0 L per kg). The tank therefore needs room for both:

Water needed (gal) = planned load (lb) ÷ rate (lb/gal) Displaced (gal) = 0.12 × planned load (lb) Tank space (gal) = water needed + displaced

Oxygen used by the fish. This is a rough estimate. Fish oxygen use varies a lot with species, size, temperature and stress. Adult fish usually use about 200–500 mg O₂ per kg per hour (Boyd, Global Seafood Alliance). Small fish use more per kg, and the rate roughly doubles for a 10 °C rise in temperature. The default is 500 mg/kg/h, the top of that range, because hauled fish are stressed.

O₂ (kg) = biomass (kg) × rate (mg/kg/h) × hours ÷ 1,000,000 Gas volume (L at 21 °C, 1 atm) = O₂ (kg) × 754.6

The gas conversion uses the ideal gas law: 1 kg of O₂ is 31.25 mol (molar mass 31.999 g/mol), and 1 mol occupies 24.15 L at 21.1 °C (70 °F) and 1 atm. This is only what the fish absorb. Diffusers in a hauling tank let much of the gas escape at the surface, so the cylinder or liquid-oxygen supply must hold several times this amount. Size it from your diffuser's flow rate and the trip time, and keep a reserve. Keep dissolved oxygen at or above 6 mg/L the whole trip (SRAC 390 and 392). See the Dissolved Oxygen calculator for saturation values.

Salt. Adding salt reduces the difference in salt content between the water and the fish's blood. This eases osmotic stress during handling. SRAC 392 lists rates of 0.05 to 1% (500–10,000 mg/L, 0.4–8 lb per 100 gal), with the lower rates for freshwater fish. The Texas catfish guide uses 0.5% for catfish hauling water. Use non-iodized salt.

Salt (kg) = water (L) × salt % ÷ 100

The Salt Addition calculator covers dosing salt in more detail.

Tempering. SRAC 392 advises tempering fish for at least 20 minutes for every 10 °F of difference between the hauling water and the receiving water. When the difference is more than 10 °F, or the pH differs by 1 unit or more, mix receiving water into the hauling tank gradually.

Tempering time (min) ≥ 20 × |Thaul − Treceiving| (°F) ÷ 10

Fish length to weight

If you enter a length, the calculator estimates weight from the channel catfish standard-weight equation (Brown et al., 1995), with total length TL in mm and weight W in g. Standard weight describes a fish in good condition. Well-fed farm fish are often heavier, so weighing a sample is more accurate. This option only makes sense for channel catfish.

log10 W = −5.800 + 3.294 × log10 TL

Example

A truck tank holds 500 US gallons of water. You want to haul 12,000 channel catfish stockers averaging 0.1 lb each (10 per pound), in water at 75 °F, on a 10-hour trip with bottled oxygen.

  • 10 fish per pound lies between the 4 and 50 per pound rows: on a log scale it is 36.3% of the way from 4 to 50. The 8-hour rate is 5.00 + (3.45 − 5.00) × 0.363 = 4.438 lb/gal. The 12-hour rate is 4.10 + (2.50 − 4.10) × 0.363 = 3.520 lb/gal. Halfway between them, for 10 hours, the rate is 3.979 lb/gal at 65 °F.
  • 75 °F is 10 °F warmer than 65 °F, so fT = 0.75 and the safe rate is 3.979 × 0.75 = 2.984 lb/gal (0.358 kg/L).
  • Max load = 2.984 × 500 = 1,492 lb (677 kg), or about 14,919 fish.
  • The planned 12,000 fish weigh 1,200 lb, which is 80% of the safe maximum. They need 1,200 ÷ 2.984 = 402 gal of water. They displace 0.12 × 1,200 = 144 gal, so they take up about 546 gal of tank space.
  • Oxygen: 544 kg × 500 mg/kg/h × 10 h = 2.72 kg of O₂, about 2,054 L (72.5 ft³) of gas actually absorbed by the fish.
  • Salt at 0.5% in 500 gal (1,893 L) is 9.46 kg (20.9 lb). If the receiving pond is at 80 °F, temper the fish for at least 10 minutes.

Before and during the trip

  • Withhold feed for at least a day before hauling (SRAC 3903; WSU Extension). Fish with empty guts use less oxygen and foul the water less.
  • Haul cool when possible. SRAC 393 notes that catfish haul well and are easier to temper at about 60 °F in summer and 45–50 °F in winter. SRAC 392 notes that ice at about ½ lb per gallon lowers water temperature by about 10 °F. Use ice made from chlorine-free water.
  • Monitor dissolved oxygen throughout the trip, not just at loading, and keep it at 6 mg/L or more.
  • Temper on arrival as described above, and check pH as well as temperature.

Limitations and assumptions

  • The catfish table dates from the 1970s–80s. Commercial haulers often carry food-size catfish at 5–10 lb/gal on short 1–4 hour trips (SRAC 390), but that is outside this table. Your fish's condition, water chemistry and equipment can make the true safe load higher or lower.
  • The 25%-per-10 °F rule and the 4 lb/gal air-only figure are rules of thumb from extension guides, not physiological models.
  • Rates for other species (tilapia, trout, baitfish, hybrid striped bass and others) differ a lot. Use a species-specific source, such as SRAC 392/393 for warmwater species or Piper et al. (1982) for salmonids, and enter that rate in custom mode.
  • The oxygen figure is an estimate. Size the oxygen supply with a margin and measure DO.
