How the fixture layout calculator works

The calculator uses the lumen method from interior lighting design, with photons instead of lumens. Every fixture sends out a known number of photosynthetic photons per second (its PPF, in µmol/s). Only part of them ends up on the plants. Some miss the canopy and hit walls, aisles or the floor. Fixtures also lose output as they age and collect dust. Two factors cover these losses:

  • Utilization factor (called the coefficient of utilization, CU, in lighting design): the share of the fixture's photons that land on the canopy, including light that bounces back off the walls.
  • Maintenance factor (part of the light loss factor, LLF): how much of its new output a fixture still gives between cleanings and late in its life.

Divide the photons that reach the canopy by the canopy area and you get the average PPFD. The calculator turns this around: it finds how many fixtures give your target PPFD, rounds up, and arranges them in a grid that suits the shape of the room. It then shows the PPFD and DLI you actually get, the spacings, the power use and the cost.

If you set the target as a DLI, the calculator first converts it to the PPFD needed during your photoperiod (the same formula as the Daily Light Integral calculator). If you only have a lux meter, convert your readings with the Light Conversions calculator first.

Why it matters for hydroponics and aquaponics

  • Lighting is often the biggest running cost of an indoor farm or a lit greenhouse. Buying one fixture too many costs money every day. One too few means slower crops.
  • The fixture count, power density (W/m²) and running cost let you compare LED, HPS and fluorescent options, or a brighter light for fewer hours against a dimmer one for longer.
  • Spacing and hanging height decide how evenly the light falls on the rafts, NFT channels or media beds. Uneven light gives uneven crops.

Formulas

Grow area A = length × width. Target PPFD from a target DLI and a photoperiod of h hours:

PPFDtarget = DLI × 1,000,000 ÷ (h × 3600)

Number of fixtures (PPF in µmol/s, CU and MF as fractions), rounded up to a whole fixture:

N = ⌈ PPFDtarget × A ÷ (PPF × CU × MF) ⌉

The grid has r rows (across the width) and c columns (along the length) with r × c ≥ N. The calculator tries every column count and picks the grid with the fewest extra fixtures and the most even spacing in both directions. With M = r × c fixtures:

Spacing along length sL = length ÷ c Spacing across width sW = width ÷ r Center to wall = s ÷ 2 PPFD achieved = M × PPF × CU × MF ÷ A DLI achieved = PPFD × h × 3600 ÷ 1,000,000 Photon efficacy = PPF ÷ input power (µmol/J) Total power = M × input power Energy per day (kWh) = total power (W) × h ÷ 1000 Cost = energy × price per kWh

The light map

The heat map is approximate. Each fixture is split into a 4 × 4 grid of small sources across its footprint, and each source is treated as a Lambertian emitter: it shines straight down and its intensity falls off with the cosine of the angle. The PPFD that one source of photon flux Φ gives on the canopy at horizontal distance r, from height H, is:

PPFD = (Φ ÷ π) × H² ÷ (H² + r²)²

The map adds up all sources, then scales the whole picture so that its average equals the PPFD from the formula above. Real fixtures have lenses and reflectors that make their beam narrower or wider, and walls reflect light back onto the edges of the canopy. Use the fixture maker's PPFD map or a quantum sensor for the final layout.

The model also gives the share of photons that would land directly on the canopy if the walls absorbed everything (“photons landing directly”). This is calculated exactly for each small source with the standard view-factor formula for a rectangle parallel to a point source. It is a useful lower bound for the utilization factor: white or reflective walls send part of the rest back, so the real CU is usually somewhat higher.

Rules of thumb

These are rules of thumb, not standards. Use the values from your fixture's photometric report if you have them.

Example (the default values)

A 6.5 m × 4 m grow room (26 m²) for a fruiting crop. Target 600 µmol/m²/s for 12 hours a day. LED bar fixtures with 1700 µmol/s and 680 W, 1.1 m × 1.1 m, hung 1.1 m above the canopy. Utilization factor 85%, maintenance factor 90%, electricity 0.15 per kWh.

Photons per fixture on canopy = 1700 × 0.85 × 0.90 = 1300.5 µmol/s N = ⌈ 600 × 26 ÷ 1300.5 ⌉ = ⌈ 11.995 ⌉ = 12 fixtures Grid: 3 rows × 4 columns = 12 fixtures Spacing: 6.5 ÷ 4 = 1.625 m along the length, 4 ÷ 3 = 1.333 m across Center to wall: 0.8125 m and 0.667 m (shown as 0.813 m × 0.667 m) Spacing ÷ height = 1.625 ÷ 1.1 = 1.48 PPFD achieved = 12 × 1300.5 ÷ 26 = 600.2 µmol/m²/s DLI = 600.2 × 12 × 0.0036 = 25.9 mol/m²/day Efficacy = 1700 ÷ 680 = 2.5 µmol/J Total power = 12 × 680 = 8160 W → 8160 ÷ 26 = 313.8 W/m² (29.2 W/ft²) Energy = 8.16 kW × 12 h = 97.92 kWh/day, 2980.7 kWh/month, 35,740.8 kWh/year Cost = 97.92 × 0.15 = 14.69 per day, 447.10 per month, 5361.12 per year

A DLI of about 26 mol/m²/day is in the 20–30 mol/m²/day rule-of-thumb range for tomatoes and other fruiting crops (Stallknecht 2025; see the DLI calculator). A spacing-to-height ratio of 1.48 is just inside the rule-of-thumb limit, and the light map shows the expected fall-off toward the walls and corners. With the walls absorbing everything, the model puts about 68% of the photons directly on the canopy, so the 85% utilization factor assumes reflective walls.

Limitations and assumptions

  • Average, not every spot. The lumen method gives the average PPFD over the whole area. Edges and corners get less light than the middle unless the walls are reflective or fixtures are added along the edges.
  • The utilization factor is the big unknown. It depends on the fixture's beam, the mounting height, the room shape and the wall finish. A fixture maker's photometric files, a lighting simulation or a quantum-sensor survey of a test bay give a much better number than a rule of thumb.
  • Simple light map. The Lambertian model ignores lenses, reflectors, shading by fixtures and plants, and wall reflections (which are spread evenly when the map is scaled). Treat the map as a guide to where the dim spots will be, not a measurement.
  • Electric light only. In a greenhouse, sunlight adds to the DLI. Work out how much supplemental light you need first, for example with monthly DLI maps (Faust & Logan 2018), and enter only the shortfall.
  • Datasheet values. PPF and wattage claims vary in quality. Look for values measured in an integrating sphere by an independent lab. Both et al. (2017) proposed a standard label for plant lamps that reports photon efficacy and the light distribution, which is the information this calculator needs. Remember that PPF covers 400–700 nm only; far-red output is not included.
  • Running cost is lighting electricity only. Cooling to remove the heat from the lights, demand charges and fixture replacement are not included.
