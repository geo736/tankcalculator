How the DLI calculator works

Daily light integral (DLI) is the total number of photosynthetically active photons (400–700 nm) that land on one square meter of plant canopy in one day. It is measured in moles of photons per square meter per day (mol/m²/day). Torres & Lopez (Purdue Extension) compare it to a rain gauge: a PAR meter reading (PPFD, in µmol/m²/s) is like the rainfall rate at one moment, while DLI is the total collected over the whole day.

The calculator multiplies light intensity by the number of seconds the light is on. If you enter lux, foot-candles or W/m², it first converts the reading to PPFD with an approximate factor for the light source you pick. It then compares the DLI with a typical range for the crop you chose, and works out the PPFD (or the hours) you would need to reach your target DLI.

Why DLI matters in hydroponics and aquaponics

Plant growth, yield and quality track the total light received per day much more closely than the brightness at any one moment. Faust & Logan (2018) review how DLI affects rooting, growth, flowering and quality across many crops. In practice DLI helps you:

  • choose how bright to run grow lights over rafts, NFT channels or media beds, and for how long;
  • decide when a greenhouse needs supplemental light (short winter days) or shade (bright summer days);
  • trade intensity for hours: the same DLI can come from a dimmer light on for longer, which often means smaller, cheaper fixtures;
  • avoid too much light on sensitive crops, e.g. lettuce, where high DLI raises the risk of tipburn.

Formulas

Constant light (grow lights at a fixed level, PPFD in µmol/m²/s, photoperiod h in hours):

DLI = PPFD × h × 3600 s/h ÷ 1,000,000 µmol/mol = PPFD × h × 0.0036

Sunny day (light rises and falls as a half sine wave from sunrise to sunset, PPFDpeak at solar noon, day length h). The average of a half sine wave is 2/π ≈ 0.637 of its peak:

DLI = (2/π) × PPFDpeak × h × 0.0036

Reverse calculations (k = 1 for constant light, k = 2/π for the sunny-day curve):

PPFD needed = DLItarget ÷ (k × h × 0.0036) Hours needed = DLItarget ÷ (k × PPFD × 0.0036)

Converting lux, foot-candles and W/m² to PPFD

Lux and foot-candles measure light as the human eye sees it; plants respond to photons across 400–700 nm. The conversion depends on the spectrum of the lamp, so the factors below are approximate. A PAR (quantum) meter is always the better instrument. The W/m² factors apply only to PAR-band radiation (400–700 nm). A pyranometer or weather-station “solar radiation” reading covers all solar wavelengths and cannot be used directly.

PPFD = lux × flux PPFD = foot-candles × 10.764 × flux PPFD = W/m² (PAR) × fW

Sunlight, HPS, metal halide and fluorescent factors are from Thimijan & Heins (1983), the values also tabulated by Apogee Instruments. *White LEDs vary with colour temperature and colour rendering. The lux factor is Sharakshane's (2018) estimate (1000 lx ≈ 15 µmol/m²/s, roughly 13–16 for typical white LEDs). The W/m² factor is our estimate from the photon energy E = hc/λ at a mean PAR wavelength of about 560 nm (560 ÷ 119.6 ≈ 4.7 µmol/J). Do not use a lux meter under red/blue (“blurple”) LEDs. Lux meters barely respond to deep red and blue light, so the reading badly underestimates PPFD. 1 foot-candle = 1 lm/ft² = 10.764 lux exactly.

Crop DLI ranges

These are rules of thumb, not exact requirements. The best DLI depends on cultivar, temperature, CO₂, growth stage and how fast you want the crop to grow. Stallknecht (2025) notes that the “correct” DLI is somewhat subjective.

Crop groupDLI (mol/m²/day)Basis
Lettuce & leafy greens12–17Stallknecht 2025 (lettuce 12–17); Purdue HO-303-W gives 15–17 for lettuce. Higher DLI raises tipburn risk in sensitive cultivars.
Basil & culinary herbs15–25Stallknecht 2025 (basil). Other herbs may need less.
Tomato & fruiting crops20–30Stallknecht 2025 (tomato 20–30); HO-303-W: 20–30 or more. Peppers and cucumbers are usually grouped here.
Vegetable transplants / seedlings10–12Purdue HO-303-W: acceptable; more light gives faster growth.
Microgreens6–18Range tested by Gerovac et al. (2016) for Brassica microgreens. More light gave shorter, sturdier hypocotyls. This is a research range, not an established target.

Example

Lettuce in a deep-water raft under white LED fixtures that give 250 µmol/m²/s at canopy height for 16 hours a day:

DLI = 250 × 16 × 3600 ÷ 1,000,000 = 14.4 mol/m²/day

That is within the 12–17 mol/m²/day lettuce range. To hit the range midpoint of 14.5 mol/m²/day in 16 hours, you would need 14.5 ÷ (16 × 0.0036) = 251.7 µmol/m²/s. At 250 µmol/m²/s you would need 14.5 ÷ (250 × 0.0036) = 16.1 hours. If you only had a lux meter, a 250 µmol/m²/s white-LED level would read about 250 ÷ 0.015 ≈ 16,700 lux.

For comparison, a clear day in a greenhouse with a peak of 1,000 µmol/m²/s and 12 hours of daylight gives about 0.637 × 1,000 × 12 × 0.0036 ≈ 27.5 mol/m²/day.

Limitations and assumptions

  • The sunny-day curve is an ideal. Clouds, glazing, shade cloth, structures and hanging baskets cut real greenhouse DLI, often by a large fraction. For natural light, a logging PAR sensor or the monthly DLI maps of Faust & Logan (2018) are more reliable.
  • One point is not the whole bench. PPFD falls off toward the edges of a fixture's footprint and changes with hanging height. Average several readings at canopy level.
  • Conversion factors are approximate. Lux and W/m² conversions can be off by 10% or more for a particular lamp. Use the manufacturer's PPFD map or a quantum sensor where possible.
  • DLI is not photoperiod. Short-day and long-day plants respond to the length of the night, not just total light, so flowering crops may need a specific photoperiod whatever the DLI.
  • Crop ranges are guidelines. Above a crop's saturation level, extra light gives little benefit and costs electricity.
