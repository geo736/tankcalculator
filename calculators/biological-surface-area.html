How the biofilter calculator works

Fish excrete ammonia, mostly through their gills, as they digest the protein in their feed. In a recirculating system that ammonia has to be turned into nitrite and then nitrate by nitrifying bacteria. Those bacteria live as a thin film (biofilm) on every wet surface, so the capacity of a biofilter depends on how much surface it gives them.

The calculator works in three steps:

  1. Estimate the total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) produced per day from the daily feed and its protein content.
  2. Divide that TAN load by the areal nitrification rate, the grams of TAN one square meter of biofilm removes per day, and multiply by a safety factor. That gives the biofilm surface area needed.
  3. Divide the area by the media's specific surface area (m² of surface per m³ of media) to get the volume of media. If you enter the filter vessel volume, the calculator also shows what fraction of the vessel the media fills.

Why it matters

Too little biofilm surface lets ammonia and nitrite build up, and both are toxic to fish. In recirculating aquaculture (RAS) the biofilter is usually the only place ammonia is removed, so it must be sized for the full feed ration at peak biomass. In aquaponics the plants and the wet surfaces of the grow beds take up and nitrify part of the ammonia too, so a separate biofilter sized with this calculator gives extra margin rather than the bare minimum. The FAO aquaponics manual (Somerville et al., 2014) explains the role of the biofilter and media surface area in small systems.

Formulas

1. TAN produced (Timmons et al., 2002; Ebeling, Timmons & Bisogni, 2006):

PTAN (kg/day) = F × PC × 0.092 F = feed (kg/day) PC = protein content of the feed (decimal, e.g. 0.35)

The 0.092 constant comes from four assumptions: protein is 16% nitrogen, 80% of the nitrogen is assimilated by the fish, 80% of the assimilated nitrogen is excreted, and 90% of the excreted nitrogen is TAN (the other 10% is urea): 0.16 × 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.0922. Uneaten feed and solids left in the system add to this, and the authors note the formula needs changing for heterotrophic (biofloc) systems where solids are not removed.

2. Biofilm surface area needed

A (m²) = PTAN (g/day) ÷ rTAN (g/m²/day) × SF rTAN = areal TAN removal (nitrification) rate SF = safety factor (1 = none)

3. Media volume and fill fraction

Vmedia (m³) = A ÷ SSA Fill fraction = Vmedia ÷ Vvessel SSA = specific surface area of the media (m²/m³)

Choosing the nitrification rate

The areal rate is the most uncertain input. It is not a property of the media alone: it falls with lower temperature, lower dissolved oxygen, more organic matter (BOD) and, above all, with lower TAN concentration, because at the low ammonia levels kept in fish tanks (usually under 1 mg/L) the bacteria are starved for ammonia (Rusten et al., 2006). Published measurements give an idea of the range:

SituationAreal rate, g TAN/m²/day
Conservative design, cold water (under about 15 °C) or low TAN0.1–0.3 (rule of thumb)

Commercial moving-bed filter on a warm-water tilapia RAS: 267 g TAN/m³/day volumetric rate (Guerdat et al., 2010), divided by 500 m²/m³≈ 0.5
Well-run Kaldnes moving bed, good oxygen, TAN not limiting (Rusten et al., 2006)up to ≈ 1

The default of 0.5 g/m²/day with a 1.25 safety factor suits a warm-water system. For trout, salmon or other cold-water fish, use 0.2–0.3. If your filter supplier gives a tested rate for your temperature and TAN level, use that.

Specific surface area is only half of the story. Fine media such as sand has a huge area but clogs unless it is fluidized, and the measured nitrification per m² of sand is usually lower than on plastic carriers. Check the manufacturer's data sheet for any plastic media and enter its value as Custom.

Moving-bed fill fraction

Moving-bed carriers must stay free to circulate. Rusten et al. (2006) recommend keeping the carrier fill below 70% of the reactor volume; 50–65% is normal in practice. The calculator warns above 70% for moving-bed media. For static media (gravel, clay, bio-balls) the vessel can be filled completely, so it only warns when the media does not fit.

Example

A tank is fed 1 kg of 35% protein feed per day. TAN produced = 1 × 0.35 × 0.092 = 0.0322 kg = 32.2 g TAN/day. At 0.5 g/m²/day the bacteria need 32.2 ÷ 0.5 = 64.4 m², and with a 1.25 safety factor 80.5 m² of biofilm. K1-type carrier at 500 m²/m³ gives 80.5 ÷ 500 = 0.161 m³ = 161 L of media. In a 300 L moving-bed vessel that is a fill of 161 ÷ 300 = 53.7%, inside the normal 50–65% range.

Limitations and assumptions

  • All TAN goes to the biofilter. No credit is taken for water exchange, plant uptake or nitrification on tank walls and pipes.
  • Steady feeding. Size for the largest daily ration you will feed at peak stocking. Ammonia excretion peaks a few hours after feeding, so a filter sized on the daily average has little spare capacity at those times.
  • A new biofilter needs weeks to mature before it reaches its full rate.
  • Nitrite. The calculation is for ammonia removal; the second nitrification step usually keeps up in a mature filter but can lag in a new one.
  • Rates and media areas in the tables are guides, not guarantees. Watch ammonia and nitrite test results and adjust feeding if they rise.
