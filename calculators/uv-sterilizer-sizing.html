How the UV sizing calculator works

A UV sterilizer is a tube with a germicidal lamp inside a quartz sleeve. Water flows through the gap between the sleeve and the outer wall. UV-C light at 254 nm damages the DNA or RNA of bacteria, viruses and parasites in that water so they can no longer multiply. What matters is the dose (also called fluence): how much UV energy reaches each square centimeter, in millijoules per cm² (mJ/cm²). The dose depends on three things:

  • How bright the light is (fluence rate, mW/cm²), which falls as the lamp ages and as the water absorbs UV.
  • How long the water stays in the light (contact time). Doubling the flow halves the time, so it halves the dose.
  • How clear the water is to UV (UV transmittance, UVT). Dissolved organic matter, tannins and fine particles absorb UV before it reaches the far side of the gap.

The calculator finds the flow through the unit, scales the dose to your flow and your water's UVT, reduces it for an aged lamp, and compares the result with the dose your target organism needs.

Why it matters for aquaponics, aquaculture and hydroponics

In recirculating systems the same water passes every tank again and again, so a pathogen released by one fish can reach all of them. UV on the return line reduces the number of free-floating bacteria, viruses and parasite stages, which limits spread between tanks (Summerfelt, 2003). Hydroponic growers use UV on recirculated nutrient solution for the same reason. UV adds no chemicals to the water and leaves no residual, so it does not harm fish, plants or the nitrifying bacteria living on biofilter surfaces. It only treats water that passes the lamp, though. It does not cure fish that are already sick, and it does not reach organisms on surfaces or in biofilm.

Formulas

Flow through the unit. If you enter the system volume V and the number of passes per hour N:

Q = V × N

Dose. Dose is fluence rate times exposure time. In a reactor of water volume Vr, the exposure time is Vr ÷ Q, so at a fixed lamp output the dose is inversely proportional to flow:

Dose = Eavg × t = Eavg × Vr / Q ⇒ Dose ∝ 1 / Q

UV transmittance. UVT is the percentage of 254 nm light that passes through 1 cm of water. Its absorbance per cm is a = −log10(UVT/100). Across a water layer of thickness g (the gap from sleeve to wall), the average intensity relative to the intensity at the sleeve is the water factor used in standard UV dose methods (Bolton & Linden, 2003):

a = −log10(UVT / 100) [per cm] WF = (1 − 10−a·g) / (a · g · ln 10) [g in cm]

Method A: manufacturer rating. Spec sheets give a dose Dr at a rated flow Qr, measured at a stated UVTr. Scale it to your flow and your water:

Dose = Dr × (Qr / Q) × WF(UVT) / WF(UVTr)

Method B: lamp UV-C watts (approximate). If all the lamp's UV-C power P enters the water and travels straight out across the gap, energy balance gives the volume-average fluence rate times the reactor volume as P × g × WF. Dividing by the flow gives the average dose. The reactor volume cancels, so you only need the gap:

Eavg × Vr = P × g × WF Dose = P × g × WF / Q (W·cm ÷ cm³/s = J/cm²; ×1000 for mJ/cm²)

This is a rough, average-dose estimate. It ignores reflection from the housing wall (which adds dose), light lost in the quartz sleeve and parts of the flow that rush past the lamp (which remove dose). Real reactors are validated by testing with organisms. Use Method A when a rating exists.

Lamp aging, maximum flow and sizing. With fEOL the lamp output at end of life:

DoseEOL = Dose × fEOL Qmax = Q × DoseEOL / Dosetarget Units needed = ⌈ Q / Qmax ⌉ Method B: UV-C watts needed = Dosetarget × Q / (g × WF × fEOL)

The calculator judges "meets target" at end of lamp life. That way the system still works just before the lamp is changed.

Doses needed by common targets

"3-log" means 99.9% of the organisms are inactivated, and "4-log" means 99.99%. Results vary between studies, strains and water types. Treat these figures as guide values, not guarantees.

Bacteria and protozoan cysts are generally sensitive to UV, and viruses such as adenovirus are the most resistant (Hijnen et al., 2006). Fish pathogens follow the same pattern. Common bacterial pathogens such as Aeromonas, Vibrio and Yersinia need only a few mJ/cm² in lab tests. However, some Flavobacterium isolates needed much more (up to about 126 mJ/cm² for one F. psychrophilum isolate; Knupp et al., 2023). That is why 30 mJ/cm² is a design minimum for bacteria, not a ceiling.

Example

A 5,000 L system with water passed through the UV once per hour gives Q = 5,000 L/h. The unit is rated 30 mJ/cm² at 8,000 L/h at 95% UVT, the water gap is 2 cm and the system water has 80% UVT.

  • Rating water: a = −log10(0.95) = 0.0223 /cm, WF = 0.950. Your water: a = −log10(0.80) = 0.0969 /cm, WF = 0.807. The UVT ratio is 0.807 ÷ 0.950 = 0.849.
  • Dose (new lamp) = 30 × (8,000 ÷ 5,000) × 0.849 = 40.7 mJ/cm².
  • At end of life (80%): 40.7 × 0.80 = 32.6 mJ/cm². That is above the 30 mJ/cm² bacterial design dose, so it meets the target.
  • Max flow for 30 mJ/cm² at end of life = 5,000 × 32.6 ÷ 30 ≈ 5,430 L/h.

The same unit would deliver only about a third of the 100 mJ/cm² rule-of-thumb dose for Ich. Controlling Ich would need about four of these units in parallel, or a much bigger one.

Things that reduce real-world dose

  • Particles and color. Suspended solids shade organisms and absorb UV. Put the UV after mechanical filtration (drum, bead or sand filter, foam fractionator). Ozone upstream often improves UVT. Clean water also keeps the sleeve clean.
  • Lamp aging. Low-pressure lamp output falls with use. One lamp maker quotes about 20–25% loss by end of life, which leaves 75–80% (Ushio). Lamps are usually replaced on a schedule (often about once a year) even though they still glow. Visible light says nothing about UV-C output.
  • Sleeve fouling. Scale and biofilm on the quartz sleeve cut UV output. Clean it regularly. Regulatory designs apply separate fouling and aging factors (US EPA, 2006).
  • Temperature. Low-pressure lamps put out less UV-C in cold air or water. Rate the lamp near your operating temperature.
  • Rating conventions differ. Some makers rate at end of lamp life, some with a new lamp, and some at very clear water (UVT 95–98%). Check the spec sheet and adjust the inputs to match.
  • Hydroponics: iron chelates. UV breaks down some iron chelates in nutrient solution, so check iron levels after adding UV. Fe-EDTA and Fe-DTPA are less stable than Fe-EDDHA (Albano & Miller, 2001).
