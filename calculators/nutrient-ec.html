How the nutrient EC calculator works

Electrical conductivity measures how easily a solution carries an electric current. In water, the current is carried by dissolved ions, so EC is the standard quick check of how strong a nutrient solution is. This calculator works the other way round: from the concentration of each ion it predicts what an EC meter should read.

Each ion carries current at its own rate. That rate is its limiting molar conductivity λ°, the conductivity per mole of that ion in very dilute water at 25 °C. Kohlrausch's law of independent migration says that, in dilute solution, the conductivity of the whole solution is the sum of each ion's concentration times its λ°. The calculator:

  1. converts each concentration from mg/L to mmol/L, using the mass of the form you entered (N for nitrate and ammonium, P for phosphate, S for sulfate);
  2. multiplies each by its λ° to get that ion's share of the conductivity;
  3. adds the shares to get the theoretical EC, an upper bound;
  4. reduces the sum by 10% to allow for ions slowing each other down at real nutrient strengths, giving the estimated EC.

This is the same ion-sum approach used by the free water-chemistry program aqion (which takes its ion data from the USGS PHREEQC database) and by the original conductivity estimate in the HydroBuddy nutrient program.

Why EC matters

Growers in hydroponics and aquaponics manage nutrient strength by EC: too low and plants run short of nutrients, too high and the salt stress makes water uptake harder and can burn roots. A predicted EC lets you check a new recipe before you mix it, find a typo in a recipe sheet (one wrong number usually moves the EC a lot), and know what your meter should read after you mix. In aquaponics, where the nutrients come from fish feed, EC is usually much lower than in hydroponics. Aquaponic systems normally run at much lower nutrient concentrations than hydroponic recipes (Somerville et al., 2014), so expect a lower EC.

Formulas

Concentration in mmol/L, where M is the molar mass of the form the concentration is reported as:

c (mmol/L) = concentration (mg/L) ÷ M (g/mol) NO₃-N, NH₄-N: M = 14.007 (N) NO₃ as nitrate: M = 62.00 P → H₂PO₄⁻: M = 30.97 (P) S → SO₄²⁻: M = 32.06 (S)

Each ion's contribution and the total (λ° in S·cm²/mol, c in mmol/L):

ECi (mS/cm) = λ°i × ci ÷ 1000 ECtheoretical = Σ ECi ECestimated = 0.90 × ECtheoretical

The ÷1000 comes from the units: 1 mmol/L is 10⁻⁶ mol/cm³, so S·cm²/mol × 10⁻⁶ mol/cm³ = 10⁻⁶ S/cm = 10⁻³ mS/cm.

TDS meters measure EC and multiply by a fixed factor. These are display conventions, not measured dissolved solids:

ppm (500 scale) = EC (µS/cm) × 0.5 (NaCl-based) ppm (700 scale) = EC (µS/cm) × 0.7 (KCl-based, "442" meters vary) CF = EC (mS/cm) × 10

The calculator also shows the charge balance (milliequivalents of cations and anions) and the ionic strength, I = ½ Σ ci zi². A real solution is electrically neutral, so the two meq/L totals should be close. A large gap usually means a missing ion or a typing error.

Example

Hoagland & Arnon's solution No. 2 is made from 6 mmol/L KNO₃, 4 mmol/L Ca(NO₃)₂, 1 mmol/L NH₄H₂PO₄ and 2 mmol/L MgSO₄, plus micronutrients. That gives 196.1 mg/L NO₃-N, 14.0 mg/L NH₄-N, 31.0 mg/L P, 234.6 mg/L K, 160.3 mg/L Ca, 48.6 mg/L Mg and 64.1 mg/L S. The contributions are:

NO₃⁻ 196.1/14.007 = 14.00 mmol/L × 71.4 /1000 = 1.000 mS/cm Ca²⁺ 160.3/40.08 = 4.00 mmol/L × 119.1 /1000 = 0.476 K⁺ 234.6/39.10 = 6.00 mmol/L × 73.6 /1000 = 0.442 SO₄²⁻ 64.1/32.06 = 2.00 mmol/L × 160.7 /1000 = 0.321 Mg²⁺ 48.6/24.31 = 2.00 mmol/L × 105.1 /1000 = 0.210 NH₄⁺ 14.0/14.007 = 1.00 mmol/L × 74.4 /1000 = 0.074 H₂PO₄⁻ 31.0/30.97 = 1.00 mmol/L × 31.8 /1000 = 0.032 micronutrients (Mn, Zn, Cu, Mo, Cl) = 0.002 Theoretical EC = 2.56 mS/cm Estimated EC = 0.90 × 2.558 = 2.30 mS/cm

A TDS meter would show about 1,151 ppm on the 500 scale or 1,611 ppm on the 700 scale. Cations (19.02 meq/L) and anions (19.02 meq/L) balance, which confirms the recipe was entered correctly. At 2.30 mS/cm this full-strength solution sits in the commonly quoted range for tomato and above the range for lettuce; half strength gives an estimated 1.15 mS/cm.

Crop EC ranges (rule of thumb)

Target EC depends on crop, growth stage, climate and system, so treat these as starting points, not rules. All but tomato are the optimum ranges from Oklahoma State University Extension fact sheet HLA-6722. The tomato figure is a widely quoted grower range that is not from that fact sheet; commercial greenhouse tomato crops are often run toward the upper half.

Limitations and assumptions

  • The theoretical sum is an upper bound. Limiting conductivities apply at infinite dilution. At nutrient strengths, ions crowd and pair up and each carries less current, so a meter reads less. The fixed 10% reduction is a rule of thumb: as a reference point, 0.1 mol/L KCl has a theoretical sum of 14.98 mS/cm but measures about 12.9 mS/cm (14% lower). Real nutrient solutions typically read 5–15% below the sum, and some recipes deviate more.
  • Divalent ions deviate most. Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ form ion pairs such as MgSO₄⁰ and CaSO₄⁰ that carry no charge. In a regression fitted to 50 measured hydroponic solutions, Fernández (2020) found the apparent contribution of magnesium lower and of sulfate higher than the ion sum suggests, and reported that a fixed-factor estimate missed one Hoagland solution by close to 30%. Recipes high in sulfate, magnesium or calcium are the least certain.
  • 25 °C. EC rises roughly 2% per °C. Meters with automatic temperature compensation report EC at 25 °C, which is what this calculator predicts.
  • Phosphate as H₂PO₄⁻. This is the main form at pH 5.5–6.5. At higher pH part of it becomes HPO₄²⁻, which conducts a little more.
  • Source water counts. Add the sodium, chloride, calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate in your tap or well water to the fertilizer ions. Undissociated or uncharged species (silicic acid, boric acid, chelated iron, urea) add essentially nothing.
  • Small constants. aqion's Mg²⁺ value (105.1) is slightly below what the Nernst–Einstein relation gives from the PHREEQC diffusion coefficient (about 105.9), and its molybdate value is high compared with the CRC Handbook. Both differences change the result by less than 0.01 mS/cm for normal recipes.
  • TDS scales are conventions. A "ppm" reading is EC multiplied by 0.5 or 0.7. It is not the actual mass of dissolved salts, which varies with the solution. Always say which scale you mean when you share a ppm target.

For precise work, mix the solution and measure it with a calibrated EC meter, then use the measured value as your reference.
